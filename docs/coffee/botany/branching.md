## Overview

Branching refers to the lateral growth pattern of the coffee plant that produces fruit-bearing wood. Coffee plants exhibit two distinct growth types — orthotropic (vertical) and plagiotropic (horizontal) — and fruit is produced almost exclusively on plagiotropic lateral branches. Branch density, internode length, and the development of secondary and tertiary branches determine productive capacity and inform pruning strategies.

## Growth Types

**Orthotropic growth:** Vertical growth from the central leader stem. The main stem grows upward, does not produce fruit, and generates lateral (plagiotropic) branches at nodes along its length.

**Plagiotropic growth:** Horizontal lateral branches growing outward from the main stem. These are the primary fruiting structures. Coffee flowers, and therefore cherries, form on nodes of plagiotropic branches — principally on secondary and tertiary branches rather than primary branches alone.

## Branch Hierarchy

| Level | Description |
| :--- | :--- |
| Primary branches | First-order laterals from the main stem; longest branches; foundational fruiting structure |
| Secondary branches | Branch from primaries; major fruit production sites; increase total bearing surface |
| Tertiary branches | Branch from secondaries; develop on mature, well-managed plants; further extend fruiting capacity |

**Node spacing (internodes):** The distance between nodes varies significantly by variety. Short internodes (Caturra, Catuaí) produce compact, dense branching and high planting density potential; long internodes (Typica) produce open, sparse branching.

## Variety Differences

| Variety | Branching character | Internode length | Yield potential |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Typica | Wide angles, open, sparse | Long | Lower |
| Bourbon | Moderate angles, bushy | Shorter than Typica | Medium |
| Caturra/Catuaí | Compact, dense | Short | High |

Compact varieties achieve higher productive yield per area through denser branching at higher planting densities (2 m × 2 m for Caturra vs. 2.5 m × 2.5 m for Typica).

## Branch Development Over Time

**Young plants (0–3 years):** Primary branch formation; establishing structural framework; limited fruiting.

**Mature plants (4–7 years):** Full primary, secondary, and tertiary development; peak productivity.

**Ageing plants (8+ years):** Older wood becomes less productive; renovation pruning or stumping is required to stimulate new growth.

## Pruning and Branch Management

Pruning manages branching to remove unproductive old wood, stimulate new growth, maintain accessible plant height, and improve light penetration and air circulation within the canopy.

**Stumping (recepa):** The main stem is cut to 30–40 cm above ground, forcing regeneration of new orthotropic shoots from the base. Complete branch renewal; typically on a 5–7 year cycle.

**Selective pruning:** Targeted removal of exhausted primary branches to stimulate new primary development without full stumping.

**Topping:** Cutting the main stem at a fixed height to control vertical growth and encourage lateral development.

**Skirting:** Removing the lowest branches to focus productive energy on the upper canopy.

## Environmental Influences

**Light:** Full sun promotes vigorous branching and denser secondary and tertiary development. Shade reduces branching density and produces longer, thinner branches (etiolation).

**Nutrition:** Adequate NPK and micronutrient supply sustains healthy branch development and secondary growth. Nutritional deficiency produces weak, sparse branching and limited fruiting surface.

**Water:** Drought inhibits new branch development and restricts secondary and tertiary formation, directly reducing yield potential.

## Key Facts

- Coffee fruit is produced almost exclusively on plagiotropic (lateral) branches — not on the orthotropic main stem
- Branch hierarchy: primary → secondary → tertiary; secondary and tertiary branches are the primary fruiting sites
- Compact varieties (Caturra, Catuaí) have short internodes, dense branching, and higher yield potential per plant than open-growing varieties (Typica)
- Plant productivity declines after 7–8 years without pruning intervention; stumping every 5–7 years renews the branch structure
- Branching architecture is a key variety selection criterion in breeding programmes aiming for high yield and harvesting efficiency
