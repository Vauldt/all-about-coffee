

# The History of Specialty Coffee

## Ancient Origins

Coffee's story begins in the highlands of **[Ethiopia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethiopia)**, where, according to legend, a 9th-century goat herder named Kaldi noticed his goats behaving unusually energetically after eating berries from a particular plant. From Ethiopia, cultivation spread to **[Yemen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yemen)** by the 15th century, where Sufi mystics brewed the drink — known as *qahwa* — to stay alert during long prayers. The port of [Mocha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mocha,_Yemen) in Yemen became synonymous with high-quality beans, and by the 16th century, coffee had spread to Persia, Egypt, Syria, and Turkey.[^2_1][^2_2][^2_3]

## Coffeehouses and Global Spread

Coffeehouses known as *[qahveh khaneh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coffeehouse)* emerged across the Near East as vibrant centres of social and intellectual life. Coffee reached Europe in the 17th century, with Venice opening the continent's first coffeehouse; the drink gained broad acceptance after [Pope Clement VIII](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pope_Clement_VIII)'s approval in 1600. These European coffeehouses became gathering places for merchants, artists, and thinkers, laying the cultural groundwork for what coffee would eventually become.[^2_3]

## The Four Waves

The modern history of coffee is commonly described through four developmental "waves":

- **First Wave (early 1900s–1950s):** Coffee was a mass-produced commodity dominated by brands like [Folgers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Folgers_Coffee) and [Maxwell House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maxwell_House), valued for convenience rather than quality.[^2_4]
- **Second Wave (1960s–1980s):** Consumer perception shifted as coffee shops became social destinations; espresso-based drinks like lattes and cappuccinos were popularised, with [Starbucks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Starbucks) playing a pivotal role.[^2_4]
- **Third Wave (1990s–early 2000s):** Coffee was elevated to an artisanal product, akin to wine or craft beer, with emphasis on [single-origin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Single-origin_coffee) sourcing, transparency, direct trade with farmers, and precision roasting.[^2_5][^2_4]
- **Fourth Wave (early 2000s–present):** Building on the third wave, this era embraces experimental processing methods, alternative brewing techniques, deep exploration of microclimates and varietals, and a push for a more inclusive, diverse coffee community.[^2_4]


## The Birth of the Term "Specialty Coffee"

The phrase *specialty coffee* was coined in **1974** by **[Erna Knutsen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erna_Knutsen)** — often called the "godmother of specialty coffee" — in an issue of *[Tea \& Coffee Trade Journal](https://www.teaandcoffee.net/)*. Knutsen used it to describe beans of the best flavour grown in specific regions with unique microclimates, a definition that remains foundational today. Alongside [Alfred Peet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alfred_Peet), who introduced high-quality dark-roasted coffee to America, Knutsen helped shift the entire industry's focus toward quality, origin, and sustainability.[^2_6][^2_3][^2_5]

## The Specialty Coffee Association

The institutional backbone of the movement came with the founding of the **[Specialty Coffee Association](https://sca.coffee/)** (originally SCAA) in 1982, which established grading standards — a score of 80+ on a 100-point scale qualifies as specialty — and professionalised the roles of roaster and barista. This formalisation enabled the direct trade and transparency principles of the third wave to scale globally.[^2_7][^2_5]

## Australia's Role

In Australia, the specialty coffee story has its own unique thread. Italian migrants arriving in the 1950s brought lever espresso machines to Melbourne and Sydney's cafés and milk bars, seeding a café culture that would grow into one of the world's most respected. Australia developed its own coffee identity — characterised by the [flat white](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flat_white), meticulous extraction, and an early embrace of single-origin beans — and today is widely regarded as a global leader in specialty café culture.[^2_8][^2_9]

| 1950s | Italian migrants introduce espresso culture to Melbourne and Sydney |
