## Overview

A blend is coffee created by combining beans from different origins, varieties, processing methods, or roast levels to achieve a desired flavour profile, consistency, or price point. Blending is fundamental to both commodity and specialty coffee, though approaches and philosophies differ significantly. In specialty coffee, blending typically aims to create complementary complexity — sweetness from one component, acidity from another, body from a third.

## Types of Blending

**Pre-roast blending:** Mixing green coffee before roasting, allowing all components to roast together. Creates homogeneous roast development and simplifies operations, but limits the ability to optimise roast profiles for each component. Works best when components have similar density and moisture content.

**Post-roast blending:** Roasting each component separately to its optimal profile, then combining the roasted coffees. Allows precise ratio adjustments, accommodates components with different roast requirements, and provides greater control. Standard practice in specialty coffee.

**Hybrid:** Some roasters pre-blend similar components (two Brazilian lots, for example) while keeping distinct origins separate, then post-blend the roasted results.

## Blending Objectives

**Flavour profile creation:** Blenders combine coffees to create specific flavour targets — sweetness from Brazil, acidity from a washed East African, body from a wet-hulled Indonesian, for example.

**Consistency:** Blending smooths variations in crop year, lot quality, or seasonal availability. When one component varies, adjusting proportions or substituting similar origins maintains a consistent flavour.

**Cost management:** Blends can incorporate lower-cost origins to achieve target pricing while maintaining quality standards. A premium origin at 15–20% can elevate a less expensive base at 80–85%.

**Complexity:** Thoughtfully designed blends create layered flavour experiences that exceed any single origin's profile.

## Common Blend Archetypes

**Espresso blends:** Designed for pressure brewing; typically balance:
- Sweetness/body: Brazil, Colombia, Central American naturals (50–70% of blend)
- Acidity/brightness: East African washed coffees (20–30%)
- Complexity/distinction: Specialty-processed or unique origins (10–20%)

**Breakfast/house blends:** Approachable, balanced, suited for daily consumption; medium roast level; chocolate, nut, and caramel flavours; moderate acidity.

**Decaf blends:** Often combine decaffeinated processing methods or origins to create a fuller flavour than single-origin decaf achieves alone.

## Blending Process

**Component selection:** Potential components are evaluated through cupping for flavour characteristics, processing method, crop seasonality, price, and roast compatibility.

**Ratio development:** Trial ratios are tested in small batches, starting with a dominant base (50–70%) and adding complementary components. Cupping guides iterative adjustment.

**Seasonal adjustment:** As crop years turn over, roasters may substitute similar origins, adjust ratios to compensate for component variation, or reformulate blends when key components become unavailable.

## Single Origin vs. Blend

**Blend advantages:** Consistent year-round flavour; complexity through combination; risk mitigation against crop failures or price spikes; cost optimisation; suitability for specific applications (particularly espresso).

**Single origin advantages:** Terroir expression; full traceability; origin character showcase; farm relationship transparency; distinctive individual character.

Modern specialty coffee embraces both — single origins for exploration of origin character, blends for consistency and application-specific profiles.

## Transparency

Specialty roasters increasingly list components and ratios on packaging (e.g., "Brazil 40%, Ethiopia 35%, Colombia 25%"). This transparency is valued in specialty coffee as a marker of intentionality and honest communication with consumers.

## Key Facts

- A blend combines coffee from multiple origins, processing methods, or roast levels to achieve a target flavour, consistency, or price
- Post-roast blending (roasting each component separately) is standard in specialty coffee; allows individual optimisation of each component's roast profile
- Espresso blends typically balance a sweet/body base (50–70%), a bright/acidic component (20–30%), and a complexity addition (10–20%)
- Blending smooths seasonal variation and maintains year-round consistency; single origins change with each crop harvest
- Specialty roasters increasingly disclose blend components and ratios
