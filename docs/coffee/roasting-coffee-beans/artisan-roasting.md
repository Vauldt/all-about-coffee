# Artisan Roasting

## Overview

Artisan roasting is a craft-focused approach to coffee roasting characterised by small batch sizes, active hands-on process management, and the development of customised roast profiles to express the individual characteristics of each green coffee lot. Artisan roasters typically work with specialty-grade beans sourced with traceability to origin, and prioritise quality and flavour expression over the high-volume throughput associated with commercial commodity roasting operations.

## Defining Characteristics

**Batch size:** Artisan roasting typically operates in the 1–25 kg range per batch, which allows close monitoring of temperature, colour, aroma, and sound during the roast. Commercial operations may process 50–500+ kg per batch in a largely automated sequence.

**Active profile management:** Artisan roasters monitor bean temperature, environmental temperature, and Rate of Rise in real time, making manual adjustments to heat application and airflow as the roast develops. Tools such as Artisan Software or Cropster are commonly used to log and visualise profiles.

**Custom profiles:** Rather than applying a single standardised programme, artisan roasters develop distinct roast profiles for different coffees — a naturally processed Ethiopian lot may receive a gentler, slower roast to preserve floral and fruity character, while a dense high-grown Kenyan may require different heat management to develop acidity and body.

**Specialty green coffee:** Artisan roasters source specialty-grade (SCA 80+) green coffees, often with direct or near-direct relationships with producers. Traceability — to farm, cooperative, or processing station — is a core value of the artisan roasting community.

## The Roasting Process

Artisan roasters typically work through three stages:

1. **Pre-roast planning:** Evaluating the green coffee's origin, processing method, density, moisture content, and screen size to develop an initial roast approach. Historical data from previous batches informs the planned profile.
2. **Active roasting:** Monitoring charge temperature, turning point, Rate of Rise, dry end, and first crack in real time; adjusting heat and airflow in response to how the batch is developing rather than following a rigid time-temperature formula.
3. **Profile analysis:** Reviewing the recorded profile after roasting — comparing Development Time Ratio, total roast time, drop temperature, and other parameters against previous batches and sensory results to refine future roasts.

## Equipment

Artisan roasters most commonly use drum roasters, which provide the thermal mass, roast time, and manual control required for craft roasting. Common brands include Probat, Diedrich, Giesen, Loring, and Mill City, in capacities from 1 kg sample roasters to 15–25 kg production machines. Fluid-bed roasters are used by some artisan roasters, particularly in experimental or home roasting contexts.

## Artisan Versus Commercial Roasting

| Aspect | Artisan | Commercial |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Batch size | 1–25 kg | 50–500+ kg |
| Roast approach | Custom profiles per coffee | Standardised profiles |
| Coffee quality | Specialty grade | Variable; often commodity |
| Consistency goal | Expressing origin potential | Exact repeatability at volume |
| Roast time | 10–15+ minutes | Often 5–10 minutes |

## Role in the Specialty Coffee Movement

Artisan roasters were central to the development of third-wave specialty coffee from the 1990s onward. By demonstrating that light-to-medium roasting could reveal origin and varietal character rather than mask it, artisan roasters shifted consumer expectations and created a market for traceable, farm-specific single-origin coffees. The craft roasting model also drove adoption of quantitative roasting tools (data loggers, cupping protocols, green coffee grading) that have since become industry standards.

## Key Facts

- Artisan roasting: small-batch (1–25 kg), hands-on, custom profile approach focused on specialty-grade green coffee
- Key distinction from commercial roasting: active real-time management vs. standardised automated profiles
- Drum roasters (Probat, Diedrich, Giesen, Loring) are the most common equipment in artisan operations
- Profile tools such as Artisan Software (free) and Cropster (commercial) are standard for artisan profile logging
- Development Time Ratio and Rate of Rise are the primary quantitative metrics used in artisan profile development
