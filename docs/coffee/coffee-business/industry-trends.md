# Industry Trends

Industry trends in specialty coffee reflect the ongoing evolution of production practices, consumer expectations, technology, and the social and environmental context in which the industry operates. Master baristas and café owners who understand these trends can anticipate change, make more informed decisions, and contribute to shaping the industry's direction.

## The Waves of Coffee: Context

The "waves" framework provides a shorthand for the industry's evolution:

**First wave (late 19th–mid 20th century):** Coffee becomes a mass commodity. Instant coffee, canned ground coffee, ubiquitous but standardised. Folgers, Maxwell House, Nescafé.

**Second wave (1960s–1990s):** Coffee as an experience. Starbucks, espresso drinks, Italian café culture mainstreamed. Emphasis on preparation ritual and drink customisation. Quality still subordinate to brand and accessibility.

**Third wave (1990s–present):** Coffee as an artisan product. Emphasis on single-origin sourcing, transparency, roast profiles that reveal origin character, barista skill, and the coffee's provenance. Intelligentsia, Stumptown, Counter Culture in the US; Hasbean, Square Mile in the UK; St. Ali and Market Lane in Australia.

**Fourth wave (emerging):** Less consensus on definition, but commonly cited elements include: data-driven precision (refractometry, pressure profiling, connected machines), science-led understanding (fermentation chemistry, extraction science), producer equity and sustainability at the centre rather than the periphery, and technology-enabled consistency at scale.

## Current and Emerging Trends (2024–2026)

### Processing Innovation

The specialty market's appetite for novelty has driven explosive growth in experimental processing — anaerobic, carbonic maceration, co-fermentation, thermal shock processing. These processes produce unusual, intense, sometimes polarising flavour profiles.

**Market impact:** Experimental processing lots command significant price premiums at auction; they have introduced consumers to the possibility of extreme fruit character in coffee. Criticism from traditionalists argues that processing-forward coffees mask terroir and move coffee toward flavoured products.

**Direction of travel:** Likely to settle into a mature segment — experimental processing as a high-end niche alongside cleaner, terroir-focused specialty, rather than replacing it.

### Robusta Re-evaluation

Robusta (Coffea canephora) has historically been associated with commodity production, cheap blends, and inferior quality. This is changing:

**Fine Robusta:** A growing movement to apply specialty production standards (selective harvesting, controlled processing, careful roasting) to robusta production. Vietnam and Uganda are leading this shift. Fine robusta can produce interesting cups with distinct heavy body, earthy-chocolate character, and lower acidity.

**SCA Robusta protocols:** The SCA has developed robusta-specific cupping protocols and Q Grader Robusta certification (see ../Q Grader Certification).

**Climate resilience:** Robusta is more heat-tolerant and disease-resistant than arabica. As climate change puts arabica production under pressure, robusta's agronomic advantages become more commercially important.

### Technology and Data

**Connected equipment and machine learning:** Wi-Fi connected machines, integrated scales, refractometers, and cloud-based shot logging are moving specialty cafés toward data-driven quality systems. The convergence of these tools with machine learning (for automated dialling, predictive maintenance, and quality trending) is a near-term development.

**Automatic and semi-automatic precision:** Improvements in bean-to-cup and super-automatic machines are narrowing the quality gap with manual espresso in certain contexts. For high-volume, consistent output in offices and self-service settings, automation is increasingly viable.

**Alternative delivery formats:** Cold brew RTD (ready-to-drink), specialty instant coffee, and nitrogen-infused canned coffee are expanding the consumption occasions where quality coffee is accessible without a barista.

### Sustainability and Producer Equity

**Climate adaptation:** The specialty industry is investing in research and collaboration with producers to develop climate-resilient varieties, sustainable farming practices, and alternative income streams (carbon credits, agroforestry) for coffee farming communities.

**Price transparency:** Pressure from producers and advocacy organisations for greater transparency in price-setting, including published data on what farmers actually receive vs. what consumers pay.

**Regenerative agriculture:** Beyond sustainability (maintaining the current state), regenerative approaches aim to actively improve soil health, biodiversity, and carbon sequestration. Some progressive specialty roasters are partnering with producers on regenerative certification.

### Competition Culture

**World Barista Championship (WBC):** The WBC continues to push technical and creative boundaries. Winning routines increasingly feature exotic varieties, experimental processing, signature beverages, and scientific precision. Past winners (Gwilym Davies, Tim Wendelboe, Sasa Sestic, Jibbi Little, Diego Campos) have shaped industry direction.

**Sensory competition innovation:** The World Brewers Cup (WBrC), World Latte Art Championship (WLAC), and World Coffee in Good Spirits Championship showcase the breadth of craft skills. Formats continue to evolve to reflect current industry standards.

**Competition as R&D:** The preparation required for competition — recipe development, sensory precision, signature beverage creation — functions as intensive product development. Many techniques, coffees, and approaches now common in specialty cafés were first showcased in competition.

### Health and Wellness

**Coffee and health research:** Ongoing research continues to refine the understanding of coffee's health effects. Moderate coffee consumption (3–5 cups per day for most adults) is associated in epidemiological studies with reduced risks of type 2 diabetes, Parkinson's disease, and liver disease. These are associations, not established causal relationships.

**Low-acid coffee:** Growing consumer interest in lower-acid coffee options — driven by consumers experiencing acid reflux or gastrointestinal sensitivity. Dark roasting, certain origins (Brazil, Sumatra), and cold brewing all reduce perceived acidity.

**Adaptogens and functional coffee:** Coffee blends incorporating mushrooms (lion's mane, reishi, chaga), CBD, or other functional ingredients are growing in the wellness-focused consumer segment. Quality of evidence for health claims varies dramatically.

### Diversity and Inclusion

**Industry demographics:** The specialty coffee industry has been predominantly white and male at its high-profile leadership levels. Initiatives like the Coffee Coalition, Re:co Symposium programming, and producer-focused organisations are working to address this.

**Producer voices:** The industry is increasingly creating space for producer voices at events and in media — moving from a model where origin countries supply and consuming countries make all the value-adding decisions, to more collaborative and equitable knowledge-sharing.

## Watching the Industry

**Key events:** World of Coffee (SCA annual), Re:co Symposium, Specialty Coffee Expo (US), London Coffee Festival, Coff'ee Fringe.

**Trade publications:** Standart Magazine, Barista Magazine, Fresh Cup, Perfect Daily Grind.

**Competition results:** WBC, WBrC, WLAC results and signature recipes are published and widely discussed; following these tracks innovation.

**Research:** SCA Research Reports, WCR (World Coffee Research) publications, and academic food science journals publish relevant research on extraction, processing, and production.
