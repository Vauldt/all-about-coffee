## Overview

A coffee buyer (also known as a green coffee buyer or sourcing manager) is a professional who evaluates, selects, and purchases green coffee on behalf of roasters, importers, or distributors. Buyers combine sensory expertise, supply chain knowledge, relationship management, and commercial acumen to secure quality coffee at appropriate prices. In the specialty sector, the role also carries significant responsibility for equity in producer relationships and for transparency across the supply chain.

## Role and Responsibilities

### Coffee Evaluation

**Cupping:** Tasting and scoring green coffee samples to assess quality, identify defects, and verify consistency against purchase specifications.

**Quality control:** Ensuring purchased coffee meets agreed specifications for grade, moisture content, defects, and sensory profile.

**Sample analysis:** Reviewing pre-shipment and arrival samples, comparing against purchase agreements to identify discrepancies.

### Sourcing and Procurement

**Origin relationships:** Building and maintaining connections with producers, cooperatives, exporters, and importers.

**Contract negotiation:** Agreeing on price, quality specifications, delivery timelines, and payment terms.

**Price management:** Monitoring commodity markets (the C-market), managing pricing risk, and timing purchases to balance cost and supply security.

**Volume planning:** Forecasting roasting needs, maintaining adequate inventory, and avoiding excess stock that would age in storage.

### Supply Chain Management

**Logistics coordination:** Arranging shipping, customs clearance, and warehousing for imported green coffee.

**Quality tracking:** Monitoring coffee through the supply chain from origin to arrival.

**Risk management:** Addressing delays, quality disputes, and contract issues as they arise.

### Relationship Building

**Producer partnerships:** Developing long-term relationships with origin partners beyond transactional purchasing.

**Transparency:** Communicating quality feedback, market conditions, and pricing to producers in a timely and clear manner.

**Technical support:** Sharing processing knowledge, quality improvement techniques, and market preferences with producing partners.

## Buyer Types

### Roaster Buyers

Roaster buyers work directly for roasting companies. At small roasters, the owner or head roaster often serves as buyer. Medium roasters may employ a dedicated buyer or sourcing manager. Large roasters operate buying teams with specialised roles for different origins or functions. The focus is on quality alignment with roasting programmes, direct producer relationships, and cost management.

### Importer Buyers

Importer buyers source coffee for multiple roaster clients, maintaining a diverse portfolio across origins. They provide cupping samples and quality information to client roasters and act as intermediaries between origin exporters and destination-market roasters. The focus is on portfolio diversity, quality range, and client relationships.

### Specialty Buyers

Specialty buyers focus on high-quality, traceable coffee — often through direct-trade relationships. The role involves extensive origin travel, producer relationship development, micro-lot identification, and a strong emphasis on transparency and sustainability. Quality takes precedence over price efficiency.

### Commercial Buyers

Commercial buyers operate at scale — volume-focused, price-driven, with standardised specifications and less individual lot differentiation. Container-scale purchases and consistent supply are the primary priorities.

## Skills and Expertise

### Sensory Skills

**Cupping proficiency:** Identifying flavours, defects, and processing methods through tasting; quantifying quality using SCA or equivalent protocols.

**Calibration:** Consistent scoring aligned with organisational standards and external references.

**Comparative evaluation:** Recognising quality differences across lots and assessing value relative to price.

### Technical Knowledge

**Agronomy:** Understanding how variety, processing method, altitude, and terroir affect cup quality.

**Processing:** Recognising how processing affects flavour development and defect formation.

**Grading systems:** Fluency in the grading standards of different producing countries.

**Green coffee analysis:** Moisture content, water activity, density, and defect scoring methods.

### Market Knowledge

**Price dynamics:** Understanding the C-market, differentials, and quality premiums.

**Origin trends:** Crop forecasts, harvest timing, and weather impacts on quality and supply.

**Supply challenges:** Political issues, logistics constraints, and currency fluctuations that affect availability.

## Origin Travel

Buyers typically visit coffee origins to cup coffee at source, evaluate lots before purchase, meet producers and cooperatives, understand terroir and processing firsthand, and identify new partners and micro-lots. Direct feedback provided in person is a significant component of quality improvement in many producing regions.

Travel is commonly coordinated with harvest seasons:

- Central America: November–March
- East Africa: October–December (main harvest)
- Brazil: May–September
- Colombia: Year-round (multiple harvests)

Modern specialty buying emphasises sustained relationships over transactional sourcing, with buyers returning to the same partners annually and investing in multi-year commitments.

## Purchasing Models

**Spot market:** Buying coffee available for immediate delivery; faster but less relationship-intensive, with higher risk of inconsistency.

**Forward contracts:** Committing to purchase future crops with price agreed months in advance; locks in supply and pricing but requires accurate forecasting.

**Direct trade:** Purchasing directly from producers or cooperatives; higher transparency, better producer pricing, and greater quality control at origin — but logistically complex.

**Relationship coffee:** Long-term partnerships with specific producers involving multi-year commitments, collaborative quality improvement, and price stability for both parties.

## Quality Specifications

### Coffee Grade

| Grade | SCA Score | Notes |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Specialty | 80+ | Minimal defects; meets SCA specialty standards |
| Premium/fine | 75–79 | Above commodity; below specialty threshold |
| Commercial | Below 75 | Commodity market |

### Physical Specifications

Typical parameters assessed on green coffee purchases:
- Screen size (bean size classification)
- Moisture content (10–12.5% is typical)
- Water activity (below 0.70 aw)
- Defect count
- Bean density

## Challenges

**Quality variability:** Coffee quality varies by lot, crop year, processing, and storage — buyers must manage inconsistency across contracts.

**Price volatility:** C-market fluctuations create financial risk and complicate pricing decisions for both buyers and producers.

**Supply chain disruptions:** Shipping delays, port congestion, and weather events affect coffee availability and timing.

**Information asymmetry:** Limited visibility into production practices, processing quality, or lot traceability is common in parts of the commodity supply chain.

**Sustainability balance:** Balancing quality requirements, fair producer pricing, and commercial viability requires ongoing compromise and long-term thinking.

## Career Path

Entry points into coffee buying typically include roastery assistant or production roles, barista positions with cupping exposure, quality control testing, and importer sample coordination. Development involves formal training through programmes such as the SCA Coffee Skills Programme, cupping practice and calibration, supply chain exposure, and building origin relationships over multiple seasons. Senior roles include head buyer, sourcing director, quality director, and sustainability manager.

## Key Facts

- Coffee buyers evaluate, select, and purchase green coffee using sensory assessment, supply chain management, and producer relationships
- Specialty buyers prioritise quality and direct-trade relationships; commercial buyers focus on volume, consistency, and price efficiency
- Origin travel — timed to harvest seasons — is a core part of the role for specialty and importer buyers
- Four main purchasing models: spot market, forward contracts, direct trade, and long-term relationship buying
- SCA specialty grade requires a cupping score of 80+ points and minimal defects
- Buyers hold significant supply chain influence: quality feedback, pricing decisions, and relationship approach all directly affect producer livelihoods
