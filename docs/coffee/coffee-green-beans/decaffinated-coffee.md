# Decaffeinated Coffee

## Overview

Decaffeinated coffee (decaf) is coffee from which most of the naturally occurring caffeine has been removed through a chemical or physical extraction process applied to the green bean before roasting. Various methods — including the Swiss Water Process, solvent-based methods using ethyl acetate or methylene chloride, and supercritical CO₂ extraction — achieve caffeine reduction while attempting to preserve flavour precursors. Regulatory standards in most markets require that decaffeinated coffee retain no more than 0.1% caffeine by dry weight (EU standard) or that at least 97% of original caffeine be removed (US standard), meaning trace caffeine always remains.

## Decaffeination Methods

### Swiss Water Process (SWP)

The Swiss Water Process uses only water to remove caffeine. Green beans are soaked in hot water, which extracts caffeine and flavour compounds; the water is then passed through activated carbon filters sized to trap caffeine molecules while allowing flavour compounds to remain in solution (Green Coffee Extract — GCE). Subsequent batches of coffee are soaked in the GCE, which is already saturated with flavour compounds, so only caffeine migrates out. The process is entirely chemical-free and is certified organic. It produces the highest moisture content in the processed beans and is associated with some flavour loss, though quality has improved significantly with modern SWP. Conducted primarily by the Swiss Water Decaffeinated Coffee Company in Canada.

### Solvent-Based Methods

**Ethyl acetate (EA):** A naturally occurring ester found in fruit and fermented products; used as a solvent to selectively bond with caffeine molecules and carry them out of the bean. Often marketed as "natural" decaffeination because ethyl acetate can be derived from sugar cane. The sugar cane EA process is common in Colombia and Central America. Retains more flavour precursors than SWP in some assessments.

**Methylene chloride (MC):** A more selective solvent; highly effective at caffeine extraction with less flavour compound loss. Subject to regulatory restrictions on residue levels in some markets; not approved for use in all countries.

### Supercritical CO₂

Supercritical CO₂ (carbon dioxide at a temperature and pressure above its critical point, where it behaves as both liquid and gas) is a highly selective solvent for caffeine. The method removes caffeine with minimal impact on other compounds, producing the highest-quality decaffeinated green coffee. Commercially limited by the cost of high-pressure extraction infrastructure.

## Flavour Impact

All decaffeination methods alter the green bean's chemical composition and physical structure to some degree. Swiss Water and solvent methods produce beans with weakened cell structure, higher moisture content, and some flavour precursor loss. Supercritical CO₂ produces the least-altered bean. The roasting process for decaf requires specific adjustments — particularly lower charge temperature and reduced drop temperature — because decaffeinated beans brown faster than equivalent caffeinated coffees.

Specialty-grade decaffeinated coffee, particularly from CO₂ or high-quality SWP processes applied to excellent green coffees, can closely approximate the flavour complexity of the same origin in caffeinated form.

## Key Facts

- Decaffeinated coffee has 97%+ of caffeine removed (US standard); EU standard limits residual caffeine to ≤0.1% by dry weight
- Main methods: Swiss Water Process (water only, chemical-free), ethyl acetate (natural solvent), methylene chloride, supercritical CO₂
- Swiss Water Process: highest moisture content in processed beans; some flavour compound loss; certified organic
- Supercritical CO₂: most selective; best flavour preservation; limited by equipment cost
- Decaf green coffee browns faster during roasting; requires adjusted profile (lower charge and drop temperatures)
