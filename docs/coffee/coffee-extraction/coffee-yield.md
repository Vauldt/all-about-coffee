# Coffee Yield

## Overview

**Coffee yield**, also called **extraction yield (EY)**, is the percentage of a coffee dose's dry mass that dissolves into the brewed beverage during extraction. It is the primary measure of how thoroughly coffee has been extracted, and together with Total Dissolved Solids (TDS) it forms the two-axis foundation of the [Brewing Control Chart](../coffee-brewing-science/brewing-control-chart.md).

## Definition

> **Extraction yield (EY%)** — the proportion of soluble material removed from the coffee grounds, expressed as a percentage of the original dry dose.

Hot water acts as a solvent, progressively dissolving hundreds of chemical compounds from the ground coffee bed. Not all of the bean's mass is soluble — only approximately 28–30% of a roasted coffee bean can dissolve under typical brewing conditions. Extraction yield describes how much of that soluble fraction has actually been transferred to the cup.

## Formula

$$
\text{EY\%} = \frac{\text{Brewed Coffee Weight (g)} \times \text{TDS\%}}{\text{Dose (g)}} \times 100
$$

**Example:**
- Dose: 18 g
- Brewed coffee in cup: 300 g
- TDS measured by refractometer: 1.35%

$$
\text{EY\%} = \frac{300 \times 0.0135}{18} \times 100 = 22.5\%
$$

TDS is measured using a **refractometer**, which reads the refractive index of the brewed liquid and converts it to a percentage of dissolved solids.

## Target Ranges

The [Specialty Coffee Association (SCA)](../specialty-coffee/specialty-coffee-association-sca.md) defines the following target zones as part of its **Golden Cup Standard**:

| Brew Method | Extraction Yield | TDS (Strength) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Filter / drip | 18–22% | 1.15–1.35% |
| Espresso | 18–22% | 8–12% |
| Cold brew | 18–22% | Varies by dilution |

These ranges are plotted on the [Brewing Control Chart](../coffee-brewing-science/brewing-control-chart.md), originally developed by MIT chemist E. E. Lockhart in the 1950s and later adopted by the SCAA. The chart maps TDS (strength) on the Y-axis against EY% on the X-axis, with the ideal zone occupying the centre.

Recent UC Davis research (Guinard, 2023) found that consumer-acceptable extraction is broader than 18–22%, and identified two distinct preference clusters — suggesting that personal taste, roast level, and grinder quality all influence what constitutes an optimal yield for a given context.

## Extraction Sequence

Compounds do not extract simultaneously. They dissolve in a broadly predictable sequence:

1. **Acids and ionic compounds** — dissolve first; contribute brightness and fruit notes
2. **Caffeine and alkaloids** — extract next; add bitterness and stimulant effect
3. **Sugars and carbohydrates** — follow; provide sweetness and body
4. **Oils and lipids** — extract more slowly; contribute mouthfeel and aroma
5. **Plant cellular material and fibre** — extract last; can add astringency if over-extracted

This sequence explains why a very short extraction is bright and sour (acids dominate) while a very long extraction becomes bitter and astringent (late-extracting compounds dominate).

## Under-Extraction vs Over-Extraction

| | Under-Extraction (<18%) | Over-Extraction (>22%) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Dominant flavours | Sour, sharp, salty, thin | Bitter, dry, astringent, hollow |
| Body | Watery, quick finish | Drying, heavy without sweetness |
| Cause | Too coarse a grind, too short a brew time, too low a temperature, too little agitation | Too fine a grind, too long a brew time, too high a temperature, too much agitation |
| Correction | Grind finer, extend time, raise temperature | Grind coarser, shorten time, lower temperature |

## Variables That Control Yield

| Variable | Effect on Yield |
| :--- | :--- |
| Grind size | Finer = more surface area = higher yield (all else equal) |
| Water temperature | Higher = faster extraction = higher yield |
| Brew ratio | More water relative to coffee = higher yield (up to solubility limit) |
| Contact time | Longer = higher yield |
| Agitation / turbulence | More agitation = faster, more even extraction |
| Water chemistry | Optimal hardness (50–150 ppm) supports even extraction; very soft or very hard water impedes it |
| Roast level | Lighter roasts are denser and extract more slowly; darker roasts extract more readily |
| Distribution / evenness | Channelling in espresso or clumping in filter reduces effective yield and creates uneven extraction |

## Yield vs Strength

Yield and strength are related but distinct:

- **Yield (EY%)** measures how much of the coffee dissolved — a property of the extraction process
- **Strength (TDS%)** measures how concentrated the resulting beverage is — a property of the final cup

A brew can have high yield but low strength (e.g. a very long, dilute filter brew) or low yield but high strength (e.g. a short, concentrated immersion with a very high dose). Both dimensions are needed to fully characterise a brew.

## Measurement Tools

- **Refractometer** — optical or digital; the standard tool for measuring TDS in brewed coffee. A digital refractometer (e.g. Atago PAL-COFFEE) is preferred for accuracy
- **Precision scale** — required to measure dose, brewed weight, and brew ratio
- **Brewing Control Chart** — plots EY% against TDS to identify whether a brew falls within the ideal zone

## Key Facts

- Only approximately 28–30% of a roasted coffee bean's mass is soluble under typical brewing conditions
- The SCA Golden Cup Standard targets 18–22% extraction yield for filter, espresso, and cold brew
- Extraction sequence is predictable: acids first, then alkaloids, then sugars, then lipids, then cellular material
- Yield (how much dissolved) and strength/TDS (how concentrated) are independent dimensions — both are needed to characterise a brew fully
- A refractometer measures TDS%; combined with brew ratio and yield weight, it enables calculation of EY%
