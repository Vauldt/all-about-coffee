## Overview

Pakistan is an emerging, largely pre-commercial coffee-producing nation whose cultivation history spans barely two decades of deliberate effort. The country is positioned overwhelmingly as a tea culture — chai and doodh patti are the daily beverages of choice across all regions and social strata — yet its mountainous north and northwest contain terrain that is climatically suited to Arabica cultivation: the river valleys of Khyber Pakhtunkhwa (KPK) and Azad Jammu and Kashmir (AJK) offer altitudes of 900–2,000 metres, volcanic-origin and alluvial mountain soils, and seasonal temperature variation that mirrors the conditions found in established South Asian producing regions. The Pakistan Agricultural Research Council (PARC) and a small number of provincial agricultural departments have driven exploratory plantings since the early 2000s, with the Kaghan Valley in Mansehra District and the Swat Valley emerging as the two most active development zones. Pakistan's coffee story is essentially one of potential rather than proven production: the necessary growing conditions exist, small quantities of Arabica are now being harvested and processed, but quality benchmarks, processing infrastructure, and market linkages remain embryonic.

## Country Overview

Pakistan occupies a geographically varied territory in South Asia of approximately 881,913 km², placing it among the world's thirty largest countries by land area. It borders **India** to the east and southeast, **Afghanistan** to the northwest and west, **Iran** to the southwest, and **China** to the northeast via the Karakoram Highway through Gilgit-Baltistan. The Arabian Sea forms its southern coastline, with the port city of Karachi at its hub.

With a population of approximately 240 million people as of 2026, Pakistan is the world's fifth most populous nation and the second most populous Muslim-majority country after Indonesia.

### Terrain

Pakistan's landscape divides into four broadly distinct physiographic zones, each with fundamentally different character.

The **Indus Plain** spans much of Punjab and northern Sindh — a vast, flat alluvial lowland irrigated by the Indus and its five major tributaries (the Jhelum, Chenab, Ravi, Beas, and Sutlej). This plain is among the most intensively farmed agricultural landscapes on earth, supporting wheat, rice, cotton, and sugarcane at scale. Temperatures here are extreme: summer maxima regularly exceed 45°C, and winter frost is common in the north. No coffee is grown in the plain.

The **Balochistan Plateau** occupies the southwest, covering approximately 44% of Pakistan's land area. It is an arid to semi-arid tableland at elevations of 600–2,000 m, receiving less than 250 mm of annual rainfall across most of its expanse and characterised by sparse vegetation, eroded river valleys, and extremes of both heat and cold. Limited experimental coffee planting has occurred in the Ziarat area of northern Balochistan, where higher-elevation conditions and cooler temperatures prevail, though results remain unpublished.

The **Khyber Pakhtunkhwa (KPK) and Tribal Districts** occupy the northwest, where the Hindu Kush and outlying Himalayan ranges create a deeply dissected mountain landscape of river valleys, forested ridges, and alpine zones. The major valleys — Swat, Dir, Chitral, Kaghan — run broadly north–south, channelling monsoonal moisture from the south and continental air from the Afghan plateau in winter. Elevations range from under 500 m at valley floors to over 5,000 m on the high ridgelines. It is this zone that contains Pakistan's most developed coffee-growing terrain.

**Gilgit-Baltistan** in the far north encompasses parts of the Karakoram, Himalaya, and Hindu Kush ranges, including K2 (8,611 m), the second-highest point on earth. Altitudes here are too extreme and the growing season too short for commercial coffee, but lower river valleys near the Chinese border hold theoretical potential that has yet to be systematically explored.

**Azad Jammu and Kashmir (AJK)** is a Pakistan-administered territory to the northeast, bordering Indian-administered Jammu and Kashmir. Its Neelum Valley and Jhelum River corridor contain forested mountain terrain at 900–2,500 m that shares characteristics with Nepal's coffee-growing pahad zone, and limited coffee trials have been conducted here.

### People

Pakistan is ethnically diverse, with five major linguistic and ethnic groups: **Punjabis** (~45% of the population, dominant in the Indus Plain and northern Punjab), **Pashtuns** (~15%, primarily in KPK and Balochistan), **Sindhis** (~15%, in Sindh), **Saraikis** (~8%, in southern Punjab), and **Baloch** (~4%, in Balochistan). Urdu is the national language and serves as the lingua franca across groups, though it is spoken natively by only ~7% of the population, primarily the Mohajir community in urban Sindh. English functions as the language of government, higher education, and formal commerce. Pakistan is approximately 97% Muslim, with Sunni Islam the majority faith and a Shia minority of approximately 10–15%.

Literacy stands at approximately 60%, with significant urban-rural and gender disparity. The country is a federal parliamentary Islamic republic, divided administratively into four provinces (Punjab, Sindh, Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, Balochistan), two territories (Azad Jammu and Kashmir; Gilgit-Baltistan), and the federal capital territory of Islamabad.

## History

### Ancient and Medieval Periods

Pakistan's territory was home to the **Indus Valley Civilisation** (c. 2600–1900 BCE), one of the world's earliest urban cultures, centred on the ruins of Mohenjo-daro (in modern Sindh) and Harappa (in modern Punjab). Subsequent waves of settlement included Vedic Aryan migrations, the Persian Achaemenid Empire's annexation of Gandhara and Sindh (c. 520 BCE), Alexander the Great's eastward campaign (327 BCE), the Maurya Empire (under Ashoka, whose edicts survive at several Pakistani sites), the Kushan Empire, and the Gupta period.

**Arab Islamic conquest** reached Sindh in 711–712 CE under Muhammad bin Qasim, initiating a millennium of Islamic political and cultural influence. The Delhi Sultanate and subsequently the **Mughal Empire** (1526–1857) consolidated Muslim hegemony across the subcontinent. Under Mughal rule, the region saw agricultural development, infrastructure construction, and the florescence of the syncretic South Asian Islamic cultural tradition.

### Colonial Era and Independence

The **British East India Company** gradually incorporated Pakistani territories from the late 18th century, completing the annexation of Punjab in 1849 following the Anglo-Sikh Wars. Sind had been taken in 1843 and Balochistan incorporated progressively through the late 19th century. The British period introduced railways, canal irrigation systems (which transformed the Punjab into the "granary of India"), and the administrative infrastructure that would shape post-independence Pakistan.

The demand for a separate Muslim state crystallised through the work of the **All-India Muslim League** and its leader **Muhammad Ali Jinnah**. On 14 August 1947, Pakistan came into existence as an independent dominion at the Partition of British India — a traumatic event that triggered the largest mass migration in human history, with estimates of 10–20 million displaced and several hundred thousand killed. Pakistan originally comprised two geographically separated wings: West Pakistan (the present-day state) and East Pakistan (present-day Bangladesh). Following the **1971 Liberation War**, East Pakistan became the independent state of Bangladesh.

### Post-Independence

Pakistan's post-independence history has been marked by alternations between civilian and military government, regional tensions — particularly with India over the disputed Kashmir region — periods of significant economic growth, and recurring political instability. Despite these challenges, the country has maintained functioning educational, agricultural, and commercial institutions, and its urban middle class has grown substantially since the 2000s, driving the café culture that now sustains demand for specialty coffee.

## Coffee Regions and Terroir

Pakistan's coffee cultivation is confined to the mountainous north and northwest, where sufficient altitude, soil quality, and rainfall converge to support Arabica growth. The growing zones are all within Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, with exploratory activity in Azad Jammu and Kashmir. No commercial-scale coffee has been established in Punjab, Sindh, or Balochistan.

| Region | Province | Altitude Range | Primary Terroir Character |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Kaghan Valley | Khyber Pakhtunkhwa | 900–1,500 m (lower–mid valley) | Alluvial mountain soils; Kunhar River valley; cool monsoon-fed climate; well-drained slopes; forested ridges moderating temperature |
| Swat Valley | Khyber Pakhtunkhwa | 800–1,600 m (Mingora region and upper valley) | Swat River system; seasonal monsoon rainfall; rich alluvial and loam soils; moderate humidity; cultural agricultural heritage |
| Neelum Valley | Azad Jammu and Kashmir | 900–1,800 m | Himalayan foothills; high rainfall; dense broadleaf forests; experimental stage only |
| Northern Balochistan (Ziarat) | Balochistan | 1,800–2,400 m | Dryland high plateau; semi-arid; very limited experimental planting; outcome unverified at scale |

The two developed regions — **Kaghan Valley** and **Swat Valley** — share key terroir characteristics: mountain soils of high organic content, reliable summer monsoon rainfall (600–900 mm concentrated June–September), cool nights throughout the growing season, and forest-adjacent microclimates that moderate extremes of temperature and humidity. These conditions are comparable to medium-altitude growing zones in Nepal and the lower Nilgiris of India. The critical difference from those established origins is the absence of developed processing infrastructure, trained agronomic extension workers, and cupping or grading facilities at origin.

## Major Varieties

Pakistan's coffee sector has not yet developed regionally adapted varieties or named selections. Introductions have been driven by PARC and provincial agriculture departments, with planting material sourced from neighbouring and comparable origins.

| Variety | Type | Notes |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Bourbon** | Arabica | Primary introduction; well-suited to mountain slopes; produces moderate yields and quality cups at altitude; widely used in Nepal and northeast India |
| **Typica** | Arabica | Present in early experimental plantings; lower yield than Bourbon; considered the benchmark for quality in mountain origins |
| **Catimor** | Arabica (hybrid) | Introduced for rust resistance and higher yield; likely present in newer plantings; cup quality inferior to Bourbon at equivalent altitude |
| **SL28 / Kenya selections** | Arabica | Reportedly trialled by PARC in KPK; quality-focused; not yet proven at scale in Pakistani conditions |

Pakistan has no domestically bred coffee varieties, no national coffee research station equivalent to India's CCRI, and no established germplasm bank. The variety diversity is limited and largely untested at commercial scale. As the sector develops, broader variety evaluation — particularly for rust resistance given the humid monsoon climate — will be necessary.

## Farming and Processing

### Farming Systems

Pakistani coffee farming is exclusively smallholder in character. There are no large estates comparable to India's Coorg or Chikmagalur operations. Individual plots range from a fraction of a hectare to two or three hectares, often interplanted with fruit trees — apple, pear, walnut — and food crops. This intercropping model is both economically rational for mountain farmers dependent on food security and naturally suited to the shade cultivation that Arabica requires.

Farm management practices are largely transferred from traditional fruit and food-crop cultivation: manual weeding, limited or no synthetic fertiliser application, and hand harvesting. The organic-by-default character of Pakistani coffee farming — a function of low input access and limited chemical use, rather than certification choice — parallels the early history of Nepal's specialty sector.

Selective hand-picking of ripe red cherry is practiced where technical guidance from PARC or NGO agricultural extension workers has reached farmers. In areas without such support, strip-picking of mixed-ripeness cherry is common, which introduces quality-limiting defects at the green bean stage.

### Harvest

Harvests in KPK valleys typically run from **October through December**, with exact timing dependent on elevation and microclimate. Higher-elevation plots at 1,200–1,500 m ripen several weeks later than lower valley plantings. The harvest season coincides with the post-monsoon dry period, which provides favourable conditions for sun-drying of processed coffee.

### Processing Methods

**Washed processing** is the most commonly employed method in the Kaghan Valley and Swat Valley, partly because clean mountain water from river systems and seasonal snowmelt is readily available, and partly because washed lots produce cleaner, more commercially assessable cups that development organisations are better positioned to evaluate and market.

**Natural (dry) processing** is also practised, particularly on smallholdings without access to mechanical pulpers. Ripe cherry is spread on rooftops, raised drying beds improvised from local materials, or clean concrete surfaces and sun-dried over two to four weeks. Consistency of drying is a significant challenge: the narrow window of dry post-harvest weather in mountain valleys, combined with limited drying infrastructure, means that naturally processed lots carry elevated defect risk.

Processing infrastructure is a critical gap across both major regions. Very few smallholders own mechanical pulpers; wet mills are absent at the cooperative or aggregator level. PARC and NGO partners have provided basic hand-pulpers and drying rack materials to pilot communities, but scale-up is at an early stage.

## Quality Profile

Pakistan's coffee quality cannot yet be described with the precision applicable to established origins. No systematic cupping data from calibrated SCA-protocol evaluations of Pakistani coffee has been published in peer-reviewed literature or specialty trade sources. Anecdotal reports from PARC agronomists and early buyers describe the potential of KPK Arabica as follows.

### Reported Sensory Character

- **Aroma:** Mild floral and fruit notes; light chocolate; green herb in lower-quality lots
- **Acidity:** Low to medium; soft; not comparable to the brightness of East African washed Arabica at equivalent altitude
- **Body:** Light to medium; clean where processing has been careful
- **Flavour:** Stone fruit (apricot, peach), mild nut, gentle sweetness; complexity is limited in current lots
- **Aftertaste:** Short; clean on well-processed samples; astringent or fermented on poorly processed natural lots

The moderate altitude of current plantings (900–1,500 m) constrains potential cup complexity relative to what higher-elevation lots might achieve. The theoretical ceiling — comparable to Nepal's best washed Bourbon from above 1,500 m — has not yet been reached at scale.

### Constraints on Quality

1. **Inconsistent cherry selection:** Mixed-ripeness harvesting introduces unripe and overripe bean defects
2. **Processing infrastructure deficit:** Absence of wet mills and raised drying beds leads to uneven fermentation and drying
3. **Limited post-harvest technical knowledge:** Moisture metres, cupping protocols, and green bean grading are largely absent at origin
4. **Small and fragmented lot sizes:** Aggregating sufficient volume of consistent quality for export grading is a structural challenge

## Coffee Culture and Popular Drinks

Pakistan is overwhelmingly a tea-drinking nation. **Chai** — a blend of black CTC tea, whole milk, sugar, and often cardamom and ginger, simmered together in a single pot — is the universal daily beverage across all regions and all socioeconomic levels. **Doodh patti** (literally "milk and leaves") is a regional variant brewed with more milk than water, producing an exceptionally rich and sweet cup, particularly popular in Punjab and KPK.

In the north and northwest — particularly in AJK, Gilgit-Baltistan, and among Pashtun communities — **kehwa** or **qahwa** is a traditional green cardamom and saffron infusion brewed in a copper samovar, served without milk. Despite the name's Arabic root (*qahwa*), this drink contains no coffee; it is a herbal preparation with deep cultural significance in mountain communities. Its presence, however, indicates an existing ritual of hot beverage preparation that coffee producers are beginning to leverage in domestic marketing.

Coffee's urban footprint has grown substantially since the 2010s:

- **Espresso-based drinks** — lattes, cappuccinos, flat whites — are standard offerings in the growing café scene of Karachi, Lahore, and Islamabad
- **Cold brew and iced coffee** formats have proliferated, particularly among younger consumers, driven by social media
- **Dalgona coffee** (whipped instant coffee over cold milk) became a popular home-preparation trend during the COVID-19 lockdowns and introduced a new demographic to the concept of specialty coffee as a home activity
- **Turkish coffee** has a niche following, particularly in KPK and areas with cultural ties to the Middle East and Central Asia
- **Affogato** and espresso-based dessert drinks appear in upmarket café menus in Lahore and Karachi

Domestic specialty roasters — still few in number but growing — have begun sourcing and promoting Pakistani-grown coffee alongside imported single-origin lots, creating a nascent origin-identity narrative around KPK coffee.

## Major Markets

### Domestic Consumption

Pakistan's primary coffee market is domestic. The country imports the overwhelming majority of coffee consumed, drawing from Brazil and Vietnam for commercial espresso blends and instant coffee, and from Ethiopia, Kenya, and Colombia for the growing specialty segment. Local roasters serving the urban café trade in Karachi, Lahore, and Islamabad are the principal intermediaries between import supply and consumer demand.

Domestically grown coffee from KPK represents an extremely small fraction of national supply — insufficient at present volume to meaningfully displace imports. The domestic positioning of Pakistani-grown coffee is primarily as a premium, story-driven product: a local origin narrative aimed at an urban specialty audience willing to pay a premium for traceable national coffee.

### Export Potential

Pakistan has not yet established any meaningful coffee export volume. The quantities currently harvested in Kaghan Valley and Swat Valley are pre-commercial, and no established buyer relationships with international specialty importers exist in the published record. Export development will require:

- Sufficient volume aggregation (typically a minimum of one tonne of export-grade green coffee to attract importer attention)
- Consistent grading to SCA or equivalent standards
- Reliable processing records and traceability documentation
- Access to export logistics, which in KPK is logistically complex given infrastructure constraints

Over a 10–20-year horizon, Pakistan's mountain coffee has the potential to reach international specialty markets as an unusual origin narrative — "coffee from the shadow of the Karakoram" — that appeals to buyers actively seeking undiscovered origins.

### Import Market Composition

Pakistan's imported coffee is sourced through several channels:

| Source | Typical Grade | Primary End Use |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Brazil | Commercial Arabica / Robusta | Espresso blends; café chains |
| Vietnam | Robusta | Instant coffee manufacture and blending |
| Ethiopia | Specialty Arabica | Specialty cafés; upmarket roasters |
| Kenya | Specialty Arabica | Specialty cafés; upmarket roasters |
| Colombia | Specialty Arabica | Specialty cafés; single-origin retail |

The instant coffee segment — dominated by multinational brands including Nescafé and Moccona — commands the largest share of the consumer coffee market by volume, consistent with patterns across South and Southeast Asia.

## Other Notable Features

### Strategic Agricultural Significance of Mountain Coffee

Pakistan's mountain coffee programme is embedded in a broader economic development strategy for KPK and AJK, regions that have faced significant agricultural and employment challenges. Coffee is marketed as a high-value cash crop that can supplement or replace lower-value crops on small mountain holdings, providing better income per unit area than wheat, maize, or potatoes without requiring conversion of forest land. PARC has collaborated with provincial governments and international development organisations (including the Food and Agriculture Organisation of the United Nations) in extension work aimed at upskilling smallholders.

### Proximity to Major Tea-Producing Neighbours

Pakistan's geographical position between India — one of the world's largest tea producers and a major exporter of Assam and Darjeeling — and China creates a cultural landscape that has historically reinforced tea over coffee. The commercial and cultural dominance of tea in South Asia is a structural headwind to coffee adoption, even as urban consumer demographics shift. Pakistan's coffee promoters frequently deploy the local origin narrative precisely to differentiate domestic coffee from a purely import-dependent commodity.

### Café Culture Growth Trajectory

Pakistan's café sector has grown at an accelerating pace since approximately 2015, driven by urbanisation, a young median population age (~22 years), rising middle-class disposable income in Punjab and Sindh, and social media culture that uses cafés as aspirational social venues. International café chains including Gloria Jean's Coffees and The Coffee Bean & Tea Leaf have operated in Pakistan, alongside a growing indigenous café sector. This urbanising demand represents the most immediate market opportunity for Pakistani-grown specialty coffee.

### Security and Infrastructure Context

Khyber Pakhtunkhwa and adjacent tribal areas experienced significant security instability between approximately 2007 and 2015, including Taliban insurgency and military operations in Swat, Malakand, and the Federally Administered Tribal Areas. The Swat Valley in particular was substantially disrupted during this period. The subsequent improvement in security conditions after 2015 has allowed agricultural development programmes — including coffee cultivation trials — to resume and expand. Infrastructure access to the Kaghan Valley (road quality, cold-chain logistics, electricity for processing) remains a constraint on commercial development.

## Key Facts

- Nascent coffee producer; commercial cultivation confined to Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, primarily Kaghan Valley and Swat Valley
- Altitude of current coffee plantings: 900–1,500 m
- Climate: subtropical highland; monsoon-fed; cool nights during growing season
- Varieties: primarily Arabica — Bourbon, Typica; Catimor introductions for rust resistance
- Processing: primarily washed; some natural/sun-dried
- Processing infrastructure: pre-commercial; hand pulpers; limited raised drying capacity
- Quality: unverified at scale; potential comparable to Nepal's mid-altitude washed Arabica
- Domestic market: overwhelmingly tea-drinking; urban café culture growing at 5–8% annually
- No established coffee export trade; all exports remain at pilot/demonstration scale
- Coffee cultivation promoted by PARC and provincial agriculture departments as high-value mountain crop
- Population: ~240 million; capital Islamabad; largest city Karachi
- Borders: India, Afghanistan, Iran, China (Gilgit-Baltistan), Arabian Sea coastline
