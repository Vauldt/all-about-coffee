## Overview

Colombia is the world's third-largest coffee producer by volume and the largest single-country source of washed Arabica, producing approximately 12–14 million sixty-kilogram bags annually across dozens of mountain departments stretching from the Caribbean coast to the Ecuadorean border. The Federación Nacional de Cafeteros (FNC), founded in 1927, is the world's most significant coffee producer organisation, representing over 540,000 smallholder families and having shaped Colombian coffee quality standards, global branding (notably the *Juan Valdez* identity), and the institutional model that other producing countries have sought to emulate. Colombia's coffee is 100% Arabica and, by FNC protocol, 100% washed — a combination that produces the clean, bright, versatile cup profile that has made Colombian coffee the world's most recognised quality origin.

## Country Overview

Colombia is a constitutional republic located at the northwestern corner of South America, bordered by Venezuela and Brazil to the east, Peru and Ecuador to the south, Panama to the northwest, and the Pacific Ocean and Caribbean Sea to the west and north. It is the only South American country with both Pacific and Caribbean coastlines. With a population of approximately 52 million as of 2026, Colombia is the second most populous country in South America after Brazil. Its capital is Bogotá, situated on the Andean plateau at approximately 2,600 metres above sea level.

### Terrain

Colombia's topography is dominated by three parallel cordilleras — branches of the Andes mountain system — running roughly north–south through the western half of the country.

The **Cordillera Occidental** is the westernmost range, rising from the Pacific coastal plain and reaching altitudes of 3,000–4,000 metres. It is geologically volcanic in character and forms the western wall of the Cauca valley.

The **Cordillera Central** (also called the Cordillera de los Andes) is the highest and most volcanically active range, rising to over 5,000 metres in its snow-capped *nevado* peaks. This range contains the backbone of the traditional coffee-growing zones — the *Eje Cafetero* (Coffee Axis) departments of Caldas, Risaralda, and Quindío are situated on its western and eastern slopes.

The **Cordillera Oriental** is the widest range, extending to Colombia's eastern frontier with Venezuela. Bogotá sits on the high plateau (*Sabana*) of this range. Coffee is grown on both slopes, particularly in the departments of Santander, Norte de Santander, and Cundinamarca.

Between the Cordillera Occidental and Cordillera Central runs the **Cauca Valley** (*Valle del Cauca*), and between the Cordillera Central and Cordillera Oriental runs the **Magdalena Valley** — Colombia's two great inter-Andean valleys. These valleys contain the country's most productive agricultural land and major transportation arteries; the upper Magdalena Valley is associated with Huila and Tolima departments, two of the most important specialty coffee origins.

In the far north of the country, isolated from the main Andean system, rises the **Sierra Nevada de Santa Marta** — the world's highest coastal mountain range, reaching 5,775 metres (Pico Cristóbal Colón) within 40 kilometres of the Caribbean Sea. Coffee grown on these slopes by indigenous Arhuaco communities is among the world's most topographically dramatic origins.

The eastern two-thirds of Colombia are lowland Amazonia and Orinoco grasslands (*Llanos*), climatically and ecologically unsuitable for coffee.

### People

Colombia's population of approximately 52 million is predominantly mestizo (mixed Spanish and indigenous descent, ~58%) and white (~20%), with Afro-Colombian (~11%), indigenous (~4%), and other groups comprising the remainder. Spanish is the sole official language; Colombia has the second-largest Spanish-speaking population in South America after Argentina.

The coffee sector is defined by its smallholder character: approximately 540,000 registered coffee-producing families, the large majority farming plots of 1–3 hectares in mountain communities across 22 departments. The FNC's cooperative and support infrastructure — extension services, technical assistance, health and education programmes — has historically provided a degree of institutional stability unusual in smallholder commodity agriculture.

## The Coffee Industry

Colombia's coffee industry is institutionally dominated by the **Federación Nacional de Cafeteros (FNC)**, a private association founded in 1927 that functions as a quasi-public body with government-supported purchase guarantee mechanisms. The FNC represents over 540,000 coffee-farming families, operates a guaranteed-purchase programme (*Programa de Compra de Café*) that provides a price floor, runs agronomic extension services through its network of local *Comités Departamentales*, and manages the *Juan Valdez* brand internationally.

The FNC's grading system designates two export grades: **Supremo** (screen size 17 and above) and **Excelso** (screen size 14 and above). All FNC-registered coffee must be 100% Arabica and 100% washed, making Colombia unique among major producers in its categorical commitment to a single processing method.

The **Almacafé** organisation (a subsidiary of the FNC) manages warehousing, quality control, and logistics. **Procafecol** (also FNC-linked) operates the *Juan Valdez Café* retail chain, which has expanded internationally and is the most visible Colombian coffee brand globally.

The Colombian coffee sector earns approximately US\$3.6 billion in export revenue annually (2024). Colombia holds six protected **Denominaciones de Origen (DO)** for coffee — Cauca, Nariño, Huila, Santander, Tolima, and Sierra Nevada — formally recognising regional quality differentiation within the national system.

## History of Coffee in Colombia

Coffee arrived in Colombia in the early 18th century, brought by Jesuit missionaries from elsewhere in South America (the precise date is disputed; the most commonly cited introduction period is around 1730). Early cultivation was small-scale and confined to the northeast of the country. The first significant commercial export of Colombian coffee is recorded in the 1830s, when small quantities were shipped through Venezuela.

The Colombian coffee industry expanded through the second half of the 19th century, initially in Santander department on the northeastern Andean slopes, and then progressively southward and westward into the Cordillera Central departments. The mass colonisation (*colonización antioqueña*) of the western Andean slopes by Antioquian settlers from the mid-19th century onward was transformative: Antioquian families brought a culture of smallholder land ownership rather than large-estate hacienda agriculture, establishing the smallholder dominance that still characterises the sector.

By the early 20th century, Colombian coffee had overtaken Venezuela and Ecuador in volume, and Colombia was establishing itself as a quality-differentiated alternative to Brazilian commodity production. The founding of the **Federación Nacional de Cafeteros** in 1927 provided the institutional framework that would underpin this positioning for the next century.

The **Juan Valdez** advertising character — a fictional Colombian coffee farmer with his mule Conchita — was created by the FNC and American advertising agency Doyle Dane Bernbach in 1959–1960 and became one of the most successful origin branding exercises in commodity marketing history. The campaign established Colombian coffee as a premium category in the North American market and contributed to the generic association between Colombia and coffee quality.

The 2008–2011 period brought a severe production crisis: a combination of aggressive expansion of Variedad Colombia and Castillo disease-resistant varieties, unusual climate patterns, and a rust (*roya*) epidemic reduced Colombian production from approximately 13 million bags annually to around 7–8 million bags by 2010. The recovery programme — replanting, varietal improvement, and an FNC-backed renovation programme — restored production to approximately 12–14 million bags by the mid-2010s.

The specialty coffee era in Colombia has been driven by micro-lot production from southern departments, particularly Huila, Nariño, and Cauca, where altitude and volcanic terroir combine with careful cherry selection and extended washed processing to produce coffees that regularly exceed 90 SCA points. Colombian lots dominate Cup of Excellence auction results globally and are prominent in World Barista Championship routines.

## Domestic Production

### Volume and Market Share

Colombia produces approximately 12–14 million sixty-kilogram bags annually. The USDA projection for marketing year 2025/26 stands at approximately 13.8 million bags — a projected 10% increase over the prior year and potentially a record. Colombia exports approximately 80–85% of production; the domestic market is one of Latin America's most sophisticated coffee-consuming cultures.

### Farm Systems

Colombian coffee is produced predominantly by smallholder families on plots of 1–3 hectares. Land ownership (as opposed to sharecropping or plantation labour) is the dominant model, reflecting the *colonización antioqueña* legacy. Farms are typically integrated into the FNC cooperative system for buying and technical support.

Shade-grown production (*sombrío*) is prevalent in the traditional growing regions, particularly in the Coffee Triangle; the shade canopy provides microclimate regulation, erosion control, and biodiversity benefits.

### Processing

**Washed (fully washed) processing** is the FNC-mandated standard for all registered Colombian coffee. Cherries are pulped, fermented for 12–36 hours, washed with water, and dried on raised beds or parabolic dryers. The washed process produces the clean, bright, versatile cup profile that defines Colombian coffee internationally.

**Extended fermentation and experimental processing** (anaerobic, carbonic maceration, honey) is practiced by specialty micro-lot producers outside the standard FNC framework, producing differentiated specialty lots that command premium prices. These methods are not part of the formal FNC grading system but are increasingly prominent in specialty export.

### Harvest Calendar

Colombia's equatorial position means most growing regions experience two harvest seasons annually — the *cosecha principal* (main harvest) and a smaller *mitaca* (mid-harvest) — providing more continuous supply than single-harvest origins.

## Varieties and Genetic Diversity

**Caturra** is the most widely planted variety in traditional growing regions and the benchamark for Colombian cup quality. A natural Bourbon mutation first discovered in Brazil and introduced to Colombia from the 1950s onward, Caturra is compact, high-yielding, and produces an excellent cup — bright acidity, clean sweetness, versatile flavour profile. It dominated Colombian planting for decades but is susceptible to coffee leaf rust (*roya*).

**Variedad Colombia** was the FNC's first rust-resistant release (1982), a complex hybrid incorporating Timor Hybrid (*Coffea arabica* × *Coffea canephora* introgression) alongside Caturra and other Arabica. Cup quality was compromised in some batches by the Robusta introgression, but it provided economic resilience. Large-scale replanting with Variedad Colombia in the 1990s–2000s was one factor in the perceived decline in Colombian quality that preceded the specialty renaissance.

**Castillo** (released 2005) is the improved FNC rust-resistant variety, developed by **Cenicafé** (the FNC research centre in Chinchiná) over many years of backcrossing and selection. Castillo has substantially better cup quality than Variedad Colombia; many specialty buyers have revised their initial scepticism and now accept well-grown and processed Castillo lots.

**Bourbon** (red and yellow) is grown by specialty-focused farms, particularly in Huila and Nariño, for its heritage cup quality — full sweetness, caramel, stone fruit. It is rust-susceptible and lower-yielding than Caturra, positioning it as a premium variety.

**Gesha (Geisha)** is grown in limited quantities on the highest-altitude specialty farms in Huila, Nariño, and Cauca, producing the most expensive Colombian lots. Colombian Gesha — particularly when grown at 1,700–2,000 m and processed with extended washed fermentation — achieves 90–93 SCA scores and commands auction prices comparable to Panamanian Gesha.

**Tabi** is a Colombian-developed hybrid released by Cenicafé in 2002, crossing Timor Hybrid with Bourbon and Typica. It combines rust resistance with the cup quality characteristics of heritage varieties and is increasingly valued by specialty producers.

## Specialty Coffee

Colombia's specialty identity has transformed over the past two decades from a national-origin commercial standard to a multi-regional, micro-lot specialty powerhouse. The key drivers have been the identification of southern department terroir quality (particularly Huila, Nariño, Cauca), investment in washed processing precision, and direct-trade relationships between international specialty roasters and individual farming families.

The **Copa Colombia** competition run by the FNC identifies exceptional lots across regions, complementing the Cup of Excellence programme. Major international importers including Mercanta, Caravela, Ally Coffee, and Nordic Approach have established permanent sourcing offices or partnerships in Colombia, reflecting the country's importance to the specialty supply chain.

The domestic specialty café scene in Bogotá, Medellín, and Cali is one of South America's most sophisticated, with a growing number of producer-owned cafés and direct-to-consumer specialty brands.

## Coffee Competitions

### Cup of Excellence — Colombia

Colombia has hosted the Cup of Excellence since 2005, when ACE (Alliance for Coffee Excellence) expanded the programme to South America. Colombian CoE competitions have consistently produced some of the world's highest-scoring lots and highest auction prices. Colombian Gesha, Bourbon, and Caturra micro-lots from Huila and Nariño have attracted international buyers paying US\$50–200+ per kg at auction. The 2022 Colombia CoE produced 22 winning lots; the programme has generated over US\$6 million for Colombian farmers across its history.

### World Coffee Championships — Colombia's Participation

Colombian baristas have a strong record at World Coffee Championship events. Colombian competitors have reached the finals of the World Barista Championship (WBC), World Brewers Cup (WBrC), and other WCC disciplines. Colombian coffees are among the most frequently featured in WBC finalist routines globally — the 2024 WBC saw Colombian lots used by multiple top finalists, reflecting the country's premium specialty position. Colombia also participates in the World Cup Tasters Championship (WCTC) and World Latte Art Championship (WLAC).
