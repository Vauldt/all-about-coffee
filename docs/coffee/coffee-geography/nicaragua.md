# Nicaragua

## Overview

Nicaragua is Central America's largest country by land area and one of its most significant coffee producers, with a growing specialty reputation built on highland growing regions at 900–1,700 metres, predominantly washed Bourbon and Caturra, and a strong Cup of Excellence presence since the programme launched there in 2002. Coffee is concentrated in the northern and central departments of Jinotega, Matagalpa, and Nueva Segovia, with Jinotega producing the most complex and internationally recognised specialty lots. The country's smallholder-dominated sector has navigated significant political disruption and emerged with a well-developed cooperative structure that underpins organic, fair-trade, and specialty export channels.

---

## Country Overview

Nicaragua occupies the centre of Central America, bordered by Honduras to the north, Costa Rica to the south, the Caribbean Sea to the east, and the Pacific Ocean to the west. At 130,370 km² it is the largest country in Central America. The landscape comprises the Pacific coastal plain in the west, a volcanic mountain chain running northwest to southeast, the central highlands where coffee is grown, and the Caribbean lowlands (the Mosquito Coast) in the east.

The population of approximately 6.5 million is concentrated in the Pacific and central zones. Spanish is the official language; English and indigenous Miskito are spoken on the Caribbean coast. The northern highlands — Jinotega, Matagalpa, Nueva Segovia — are the economic and cultural heartland of Nicaraguan coffee, with communities in these departments historically organised around agricultural production.

## The Coffee Industry

Coffee is Nicaragua's most important agricultural export and a central pillar of the rural economy. Approximately 44,000 coffee farming families cultivate around 175,000 hectares, with production of approximately 150,000–170,000 metric tonnes in strong years. CONACAFE (Consejo Nacional del Café) is the principal government body coordinating the sector, though in practice the cooperative and private exporter structure has been the primary driver of quality development and international market access.

The cooperative sector is unusually strong by Central American standards. CECOCAFEN (northern Jinotega and Matagalpa) and PRODECOOP (Nueva Segovia and Estelí) are among the most internationally prominent, with extensive organic and fair-trade certification, in-house dry mills, cupping laboratories, and direct relationships with specialty roasters in Europe, North America, and Japan. Both cooperatives have Cup of Excellence-placing members and have been recognised by the SCA and other specialty bodies.

Principal export markets include the United States, Germany, Belgium, France, and Japan. The specialty segment represents a growing share of total exports, particularly from cooperative-organised organic and fair-trade lots and from direct-trade relationships involving Cup of Excellence-winning farms.

## History of Coffee in Nicaragua

Coffee cultivation in Nicaragua dates to the mid-19th century, following independence from Spain and the Central American Federation. The Nicaraguan government of the 1850s–1870s actively incentivised the cultivation of coffee in the northern highlands, granting land concessions and building road infrastructure to facilitate export through the Pacific port of Corinto.

By the late 19th and early 20th centuries, large haciendas dominated production in Matagalpa and Jinotega, many owned by German and British immigrants who introduced both technical expertise and modern processing infrastructure. The Somoza family's political dominance of Nicaragua (1937–1979) maintained the hacienda structure; coffee wealth was concentrated among a small elite.

The Sandinista revolution of 1979 brought the expropriation of large estates and the conversion of many to state cooperatives and production collectives under the Empresa Nacional del Café (ENCAFE). The contra war of the 1980s disrupted production across the northern highlands, and the combined effects of political instability, the US trade embargo, and the collapse of the International Coffee Agreement in 1989 severely damaged the sector. The democratic transition of 1990 and subsequent land privatisation returned production to private and cooperative ownership.

The arrival of the Cup of Excellence programme in Nicaragua in 2002 was transformative for the quality sector, providing a transparent competition framework that identified the country's best producers and connected them directly with international specialty buyers. The 2012–2013 *Roya* (coffee leaf rust) epidemic caused significant crop losses across all growing regions and accelerated the adoption of resistant varieties, particularly Marsellesa and Catimor.

## Domestic Production

Nicaragua produces approximately 150,000–170,000 metric tonnes of green coffee per year. All commercial production is Arabica; no significant Robusta cultivation exists. The farming system is dominated by smallholders with plots of one to five hectares, organised collectively through cooperatives for processing, certification, and export.

Processing is predominantly washed (wet process), carried out at cooperative wet mills and estate beneficios. Fermentation of 24–48 hours followed by washing and raised-bed or patio drying is the regional standard. Natural and honey processing are practiced by specialty-oriented farms and cooperatives seeking differentiated lots for competition and direct trade.

## Coffee-Growing Regions

| Region | Altitude | Character |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Jinotega | 1,000–1,700 m | Complex, bright; fruit and floral; most celebrated specialty region |
| Matagalpa | 900–1,400 m | Balanced; chocolate, stone fruit, mild acidity |
| Nueva Segovia | 1,200–1,700 m | Bright, citrus-forward; high-altitude lots approaching Jinotega quality |
| Madriz | 1,000–1,500 m | Sweet, mild; smaller volume |
| Estelí | 800–1,200 m | Softer, lower-altitude; commercial grade dominant |

**Jinotega** is Nicaragua's most celebrated specialty department, with the Datanlí-El Diablo Biological Reserve creating a protected forested microclimate around many of the country's finest farms. **Nueva Segovia**, bordering Honduras near Ocotepeque, reaches altitudes comparable to the best Honduran growing zones and is the second most prominent specialty region. **Matagalpa** is the largest producing department by volume and provides the consistent commercial and quality mid-tier that anchors Nicaragua's export identity.

## Varieties and Genetic Diversity

Caturra is the dominant variety planted across all regions for manageable plant size and adequate cup quality. **Bourbon** — particularly red Bourbon — is the flagship specialty variety; grown by specialty-focused farms, it consistently produces the most complex and highest-scoring lots at Cup of Excellence. Catuai is common in commercial plantings. **Pacamara** (the large-bean Salvadoran hybrid between Pacas and Maragogype) is grown by a small number of specialty producers and produces distinctive high-scoring lots. **Marsellesa** and Catimor are increasingly planted for leaf rust resistance following the 2012–2013 *Roya* outbreak.

---

## Specialty Coffee

Nicaragua's specialty identity has been built primarily through the Cup of Excellence programme and the cooperative export model. CECOCAFEN and PRODECOOP have invested in cupping laboratories, raised-bed drying infrastructure, and quality training programmes that have allowed individual farms to identify and market premium lots separately from their commercial bulk production.

The specialty sector is characterised by a productive tension between volume-driven organic and fair-trade channels — which reward certification compliance and reliable quality at scale — and the high-touch direct-trade model, which rewards exceptional individual lots but involves smaller volumes and more complex buyer relationships. Nicaragua's cooperatives are among the most sophisticated in Central America in managing both simultaneously.

## Coffee Competitions

**Cup of Excellence (CoE):** Nicaragua joined the Cup of Excellence programme in 2002, one of the earliest Central American countries to do so. The programme runs annual national competitions with international jury participation. Jinotega and Nueva Segovia farms have been the most consistent CoE performers; Bourbon and Pacamara varieties dominate the top placings.

**Nicaragua Barista Championship:** An annual national competition affiliated with the World Coffee Championships selects Nicaragua's representative for the World Barista Championship. The competition is managed through the Nicaraguan specialty coffee community and has grown in profile alongside the country's international specialty recognition.

## Key Facts

- Central America's largest country by land area; ~150,000–170,000 MT/yr coffee production
- ~44,000 farming families; smallholder-dominated; all Arabica production
- Principal growing regions: Jinotega, Matagalpa, Nueva Segovia, Madriz, Estelí
- Altitude: 900–1,700 m; best specialty quality above 1,200 m
- Dominant varieties: Caturra, Bourbon; Pacamara, Catuai, Marsellesa also grown
- Strong cooperative sector: CECOCAFEN, PRODECOOP — organic, fair-trade, and specialty tracks
- Cup of Excellence programme since 2002
- 2012–2013 *Roya* epidemic: major crop losses; accelerated resistant variety adoption
- Processing: washed dominant; cooperative wet mills; natural and honey in specialty lots
