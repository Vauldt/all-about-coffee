## Overview

Bolivia is a landlocked South American coffee producer known for high-altitude Arabica cultivation, widespread organic certification, and smallholder cooperative farming in the Yungas mountain valleys northeast of La Paz. Annual production is approximately 20,000–25,000 metric tonnes, making Bolivia a small producer by regional standards; however, Bolivian specialty lots offer exceptional quality potential due to ideal growing conditions — altitude, volcanic-origin soils, and traditional Typica dominance — in the Yungas region. The origin remains significantly underrepresented in global specialty markets relative to its cup quality, a gap driven by geographic isolation, infrastructure deficits, and limited market access rather than by any shortfall in terroir or variety quality.

## Country Overview

Bolivia is a landlocked country in central South America, covering approximately 1.1 million km². It is bordered by Brazil to the north and east, Paraguay and Argentina to the south, and Peru and Chile to the west. Bolivia is one of the highest-altitude countries in the world, with the Altiplano — the high-plateau basin between the Andes' Western and Eastern Cordilleras — at 3,600–4,000 metres. With a population of approximately 12 million people as of 2026, Bolivia is one of South America's most indigenous-majority nations, with Quechua and Aymara peoples constituting the largest ethnic groups. La Paz (administrative capital and largest city, approximately 1.8 million in the metropolitan area) is at 3,660 metres — the world's highest capital city by some measures — with its city centre in the bowl of a dramatic canyon. Sucre is Bolivia's constitutional capital.

### Terrain

Bolivia's terrain divides into three distinct zones relevant to coffee:

The **Altiplano** and **Andes Cordilleras** form Bolivia's western third at 3,600–6,500 metres — too cold and arid for coffee cultivation but the source of the snowmelt water that feeds the Yungas river valleys.

The **Yungas** — the focus of Bolivia's coffee sector — are subtropical transition valleys on the Andean eastern escarpment northeast of La Paz, dropping from 3,500 metres to approximately 800 metres over short horizontal distances. The name *yungas* (from the Aymara word for warm valley) describes these sharply incised, fog-shrouded subtropical valleys where altitude, rainfall, and fertile soils converge to create exceptional Arabica-growing conditions. The **Nor Yungas** and **Sud Yungas** provinces of La Paz department are the primary coffee zones; the commercial hub is **Caranavi**, a small city at approximately 600 metres that serves as the collection, processing, and export centre.

The **Tropical Lowlands** (Beni, Pando, Santa Cruz) are too hot and low-lying for quality Arabica but support some commercial production in higher-altitude sub-zones of Cochabamba and Vandiola.

### People

Bolivia's population of approximately 12 million is predominantly indigenous (Quechua ~30%, Aymara ~25%) and mestizo (~42%). Aymara communities are the primary coffee cultivators in the Yungas, carrying traditional agricultural practices shaped by centuries of Andean farming culture. Spanish is the official language alongside 36 recognised indigenous languages.

## The Coffee Industry

### Industry Structure

Bolivia's coffee sector is organised around smallholder cooperative production, with **FECAFEB** (Federación de Caficultores Exportadores de Bolivia) as the umbrella cooperative federation coordinating regional cooperatives and providing technical support, processing services, and export logistics to member farms. Individual cooperatives include **CELCCAR** (Caranavi), **APECAFE** (Nord Yungas), and others operating under the FECAFEB framework.

Most Bolivian coffee is exported as certified organic and/or fair trade, giving smallholders access to premium pricing channels that partially offset the logistics cost disadvantage of Bolivia's landlocked position. Export requires road transport to either Chilean Pacific ports (Iquique, Arica) or Brazilian Atlantic ports — both multi-day overland journeys that add significant cost to the export supply chain.

### Export Profile

Bolivia exports almost entirely green washed Arabica, primarily to specialty and certified markets in Germany, the United States, Japan, and Australia. Organic and fair-trade certifications are the principal commercial differentiation. Bolivia does not have a Cup of Excellence programme, though the Alliance for Coffee Excellence has conducted quality assessments in the country.

## History of Coffee in Bolivia

Coffee arrived in Bolivia in the 1880s, relatively late compared to neighbouring Brazil, Peru, and Colombia. Initial cultivation was small-scale, with production remaining limited until the mid-20th century.

The collapse of Bolivia's tin mining industry in the 1980s — triggered by a global price collapse — drove rural populations from the mining areas into agriculture, including coffee cultivation in the Yungas. This demographic shift was reinforced by international development programmes, particularly **USAID Alternative Development** initiatives that promoted coffee as a substitute crop for coca cultivation in the Chapare and Yungas zones. Investment in processing infrastructure, cooperative organisation, and market connections followed.

Organic coffee exports grew significantly from the 1990s as smallholder cooperatives identified international certified markets that paid premiums above commodity pricing. By the 2000s–2010s, Bolivian specialty coffee — particularly high-altitude Typica lots from Caranavi and Nor Yungas — had attracted attention from specialty buyers in Europe and Japan seeking clean, balanced, distinctive Arabica. Bolivia's Typica-dominant production at altitudes above 1,500 metres is increasingly recognised as one of South America's most undervalued specialty origins.

## Domestic Production

### Overview

Bolivia produces approximately 20,000–25,000 metric tonnes of green coffee equivalent annually. Production is almost entirely washed Arabica from the Yungas mountain valleys.

- **Annual production:** ~100,000–130,000 sixty-kilogram bags
- **Farming families:** ~23,000+ smallholders averaging 2–5 hectares
- **Organic certification:** ~40–50% of production certified organic — among the highest proportions globally
- **Cooperative structure:** Most smallholders affiliated with FECAFEB cooperatives

### Farm Systems

Smallholder cultivation on steep Yungas valley slopes at 1,200–2,300 metres, with average farm sizes of 2–5 hectares. Coffee is typically shade-grown under native and planted trees on terraced hillsides. Most cooperatives operate collective wet mills to which farmers deliver cherry; individual farm-level processing is less common.

## Varieties and Genetic Diversity

**Typica** is the dominant variety at 60–70% of Bolivia's planted Arabica area. Typica's well-documented cup quality at high altitude — clean, sweet, complex, with well-defined citric acidity — is the basis for Bolivia's specialty potential. The variety's presence at such high proportions reflects the relatively recent development of the sector compared to Brazil or Colombia, where Typica was largely displaced by higher-yielding modern varieties decades ago.

**Caturra** (20–30%): adopted for higher yields.

**Catuai** (5–10%): some adoption in lower-altitude and mid-altitude areas.

**Bourbon:** limited plantings; present on some specialty-focused farms.

The conservative variety landscape — favouring quality-associated traditional varieties over high-yield hybrids — is one of Bolivia's most distinctive industry characteristics.

## Specialty Coffee

Bolivian specialty Arabica from Caranavi and Nor Yungas is increasingly recognised internationally as a clean, balanced, high-value origin. The profile — bright citric and malic acidity, caramel and brown sugar sweetness, stone fruit, milk chocolate, medium-full body — positions Bolivia alongside Peru and Ecuador as an undervalued Andean alternative to Colombian or Peruvian specialty.

The 40–50% organic certification rate is one of the highest of any significant producing country, a reflection of both the traditionally low-input smallholder farming practices and the availability of organic premium pricing through cooperative channels.

Bolivia does not have a national Cup of Excellence programme, limiting international market visibility compared to countries with annual competition-driven auction cycles.

---

## Coffee Competitions

Bolivia does not currently host a Cup of Excellence programme. National-level quality assessment is conducted through FECAFEB cooperative cupping protocols. Bolivia participates in WBC-framework national barista competitions.

## Key Facts

- **Capital:** La Paz (administrative); Sucre (constitutional)
- **Population:** ~12 million
- **Coffee-growing regions:** Nor Yungas, Sud Yungas (primary specialty); Cochabamba/Vandiola (secondary)
- **Altitude:** 1,200–2,300 m (Yungas); best specialty lots from 1,500–2,300 m
- **Production volume:** ~20,000–25,000 MT/yr; almost entirely washed Arabica
- **Dominant variety:** Typica (60–70%); Caturra (20–30%)
- **Processing:** Washed (80–90% of specialty); natural and honey (limited, growing)
- **Organic certification:** ~40–50% — among the world's highest proportions
- **Cooperative structure:** FECAFEB umbrella federation; most smallholders cooperatively affiliated
- **Export markets:** Germany, US, Japan, Australia (specialty/organic channels)
