# Huila Coffee Region

**Tags:** #coffee/geography #coffee/geography/south-america #coffee/geography/colombia

## Overview

Huila is Colombia's single most productive coffee department by volume (~17% of national output) and the country's most prominent specialty origin, producing more Cup of Excellence-winning lots than any other Colombian region. Located in the upper Magdalena River valley in south-central Colombia at altitudes of 1,200–2,000 metres, Huila's combination of volcanic soils, equatorial latitude, two annual harvest cycles, and a large population of quality-focused smallholder farmers has made it the heartland of Colombian specialty coffee. Huila holds a protected *Denominación de Origen* (DO) and is arguably the single most important micro-lot origin in South America.

---

## Geography and Terrain

Huila department occupies the upper Magdalena Valley in southwestern Colombia, centred on Neiva (the departmental capital) at the valley floor, but with coffee production concentrated in the higher surrounding mountain municipalities: San Agustín, Pitalito, Acevedo, Palestina, La Argentina, Isnos, and others. The landscape is a patchwork of steep mountain slopes and river valleys on both the eastern face of the Cordillera Central and the western face of the Cordillera Oriental.

Altitude ranges from approximately 1,200 to 2,000 metres across the coffee zone, with the highest-quality specialty farms concentrated above 1,600 metres in the southern municipalities of the department (Pitalito, San Agustín, Acevedo). Soils are volcanic in origin — Huila sits near the Colombian Massif (Nudo de los Pastos), the geological junction of the three Andean cordilleras — providing the fertile, mineral-rich substrates associated with complex cup character.

The climate is equatorial highland: two wet seasons and two dry seasons annually, producing the *cosecha principal* (September–December in most areas) and *mitaca* (April–June) harvest cycles that give Colombia its double-harvest advantage over single-harvest origins.

---

## Farming Systems

Huila is predominantly smallholder: the typical farm is 1–3 hectares, operated by a family within the FNC cooperative framework. The Pitalito municipality — a major hub — is known for its concentration of specialty-oriented farms, many with FNC and third-party certifications, and direct relationships with international specialty importers.

---

## Processing

Fully washed processing (FNC standard) is dominant. Extended washed fermentation (12–72 hours) is practiced by specialty farms seeking greater cup complexity. Anaerobic, carbonic maceration, and honey methods are used by a growing number of farms producing differentiated specialty lots for direct-trade buyers.

---

## Varieties

Caturra and Castillo are the most widely planted varieties. Bourbon is grown on quality-focused farms. Tabi is used on some specialty farms. Gesha and Pink Bourbon command premium prices at the top end.

---

## Cup Profile

Huila (standard washed): medium body, medium-bright acidity, caramel, chocolate, red fruit (cherry, plum), balanced sweetness; the definition of "classic Colombian specialty." SCA 84–87.

Huila (specialty micro-lot, high altitude, extended fermentation): medium-full body, vibrant acidity, layered fruit (berry, stonefruit, orange), floral complexity, caramel depth, long finish. SCA 87–91; CoE winners 91–93+.

---

## Key Facts

- ~17% of Colombian national production — largest producing department
- Altitude: 1,200–2,000 m; premium farms above 1,600 m
- Two annual harvests: *cosecha principal* (Sept–Dec) and *mitaca* (Apr–Jun)
- *Denominación de Origen* (DO) — protected geographical indication
- Most prolific Colombian Cup of Excellence department
- Key municipalities: Pitalito, San Agustín, Acevedo, La Argentina, Isnos
