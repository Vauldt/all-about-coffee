## Overview

Guatemala is one of Central America's most celebrated coffee origins, distinguished by extraordinary regional diversity driven by the country's dramatic volcanic topography — eight officially designated growing regions, each shaped by a distinct combination of volcanic soil, altitude, rainfall, and microclimate. Coffees range from the bright, fruit-forward washed lots of Huehuetenango in the northwestern highlands to the rich, chocolate-and-spice profiles of Antigua, grown in the shadow of three volcanoes at 1,500–1,700 metres. Guatemala was among the earliest Central American countries to develop a regionally differentiated specialty-marketing identity, and **Anacafé** (the Guatemalan Coffee Association) has actively promoted regional origin specificity since the 1960s. Guatemala's Cup of Excellence programme, active since 2001, has consistently identified world-class micro-lots from Huehuetenango, Atitlán, and Acatenango that compete at the highest tier of the global specialty market.

## Country Overview

Guatemala is a Central American republic of approximately 108,889 km², bordered by Mexico to the north and northwest, Belize to the northeast, the Caribbean Sea (Bay of Honduras) to a short eastern coast, Honduras to the east, El Salvador to the southeast, and the Pacific Ocean to the south. The capital and largest city is Guatemala City (Ciudad de Guatemala), located in the southern highlands. The population is approximately 18 million as of 2026. The official language is Spanish, with approximately 25 indigenous Mayan languages also spoken by a significant portion of the population.

### Terrain

Guatemala's terrain is defined by three distinct geographic zones:

The **Pacific coastal plain** (Pacific lowlands, Costa Sur) is a narrow hot and humid strip along the Pacific coast — too warm for quality coffee, but historically important for sugar cane and other tropical crops.

The **volcanic highland chain** (Altiplano and western highlands) runs east–west through the southern part of the country, forming the backbone of Guatemala's coffee economy. This is an extension of the Sierra Madre range from Mexico, studded with volcanoes — Guatemala has 37 volcanoes, of which four are active — and defined by deep ravines, high plateaus, and intermontane valleys at 1,300–2,200 metres. The highest peak in Central America is **Volcán Tajumulco** (4,220 m) in San Marcos department. Active volcanoes **Volcán Fuego**, **Santiaguito** (Santa María volcano complex), and **Pacaya** regularly erupt, depositing ash that fertilises soils across western Guatemala.

The **northern lowlands** (Petén) are jungle-covered, sparsely populated limestone karst — home to the ancient Maya city of Tikal — and not a coffee-growing zone.

The volcanic highland zone is the entire basis of Guatemala's coffee identity. Altitude, volcanic soil fertility, distinct microclimates created by the Pacific slope versus the interior highland position, and the influence of Atlantic moisture (particularly in Cobán and the eastern highlands) create the profile differentiation between Guatemala's eight growing regions.

### People

Guatemala is the most populous country in Central America. The population is approximately split between **Ladino/Mestizo** (~56%) and **Maya indigenous** (~41%) communities, with small Garifuna and Xinca populations. The Maya population comprises 22 distinct groups including K'iche', Kaqchikel, Mam, Q'eqchi', Ixil, and others. Many of Guatemala's coffee-growing smallholder communities in the highland zones — particularly in Huehuetenango, San Marcos, and Cobán — are Maya or mixed Maya-Ladino communities for whom coffee is the primary cash crop on ancestral highland lands.

## The Coffee Industry

Coffee is Guatemala's most important agricultural export, generating approximately US$700–900 million annually and accounting for approximately 40% of total agricultural export value. The industry supports approximately 120,000 coffee-producing families and approximately 400,000 seasonal harvest workers.

**Anacafé (Asociación Nacional del Café)** is the national coffee authority, founded in 1960. Anacafé is among the most active and internationally respected national coffee organisations in Latin America. Its roles include: administering the eight regional origin designation system and associated marketing programmes; maintaining quality grading standards; operating the Cup of Excellence programme; conducting agronomic research and extension services; and representing Guatemalan coffee at international trade events. Anacafé's regional marketing initiative — launched in the 1990s — positioned Guatemala as an origin with distinct regional character long before other Central American countries adopted similar strategies.

Key export markets include the United States (the largest single buyer), Japan, Canada, Germany, and the United Kingdom. Guatemalan specialty coffee has a particularly strong position in the Japanese specialty market, where regional origin transparency and washed Arabica quality are valued.

## History of Coffee in Guatemala

Coffee was introduced to Guatemala by Jesuit priests in the mid-18th century, initially cultivated in the botanical gardens of monasteries near Guatemala City. Commercial cultivation expanded in the 1840s–1860s as Guatemala's economic reformers under president Justo Rufino Barrios saw coffee as a replacement for the declining indigo and cochineal dye industries that had dominated colonial-era exports.

The most consequential development in the formation of the Guatemalan coffee industry was the arrival of **German immigrant entrepreneurs** in the 1860s–1880s. German families established large fincas (estates) across the western highland regions — Cobán, Huehuetenango, and Quetzaltenango — bringing European capital, estate management models, and direct connections to European (especially German and Hamburg) commodity markets. By 1900, approximately 60% of Guatemala's coffee production was owned or controlled by German-heritage families, and Guatemala had become one of the top five global coffee exporters.

This German-dominated structure was abruptly transformed by **World War II**: under pressure from the United States, the Guatemalan government expropriated German-owned coffee estates between 1942 and 1944. The redistributed estates were reorganised under Guatemalan ownership, partially changing the landownership structure of the highland coffee belt.

The post-war era produced Guatemala's most politically turbulent coffee history. The presidency of **Jacobo Árbenz (1951–1954)** included agrarian reforms that affected some coffee estates; the 1954 CIA-supported coup replaced Árbenz with a military government and ended the reform era. The subsequent **Guatemalan Civil War (1960–1996)** — one of Latin America's longest — created ongoing instability in the rural highlands where much of the coffee was grown, particularly affecting Maya smallholder communities in the conflict zones of the western highlands.

Despite the civil conflict, **Anacafé's founding in 1960** initiated the modernisation of Guatemala's coffee quality infrastructure. The 1990s post-war peace period allowed the specialty coffee movement to take root: estate owners invested in quality improvement, regional identity marketing launched, and the first Cup of Excellence was held in Guatemala in 2001.

## Domestic Production

### Volume and Market Share

Guatemala produces approximately 3.5–4.5 million sixty-kilogram bags annually, representing approximately 2–3% of global coffee supply. All production is *Coffea arabica*; no commercial Robusta is grown. Guatemala is typically the second or third largest Central American producer after Honduras, and ranks among the world's top ten producers by volume.

### Farm Systems

Guatemala's coffee is grown across a spectrum of farm types:

**Estate cultivation** (fincas): Larger farms of 5–500+ ha, often vertically integrated with on-farm wet mills (beneficios húmedos). The estate model is most prominent in Antigua, Atitlán, and Acatenango, where well-known named estates (e.g. Finca El Injerto, Finca La Hermosa, Finca Filadelfia) produce individually branded specialty lots.

**Smallholder cultivation**: Farms of 0.5–5 ha, common in Huehuetenango, Cobán, and parts of San Marcos. Many smallholder communities are Maya indigenous, growing coffee on highland land plots with limited capital. Smallholders typically deliver cherry to cooperative wet mills or private beneficios.

**Cooperative sector**: Cooperatives in Huehuetenango (particularly ACODIHUE and UNAC) have developed strong international specialty relationships, providing infrastructure and market access to smallholder members.

### Processing

**Washed (fully washed) processing** is standard across Guatemala and represents the majority of commercial and specialty production. Well-developed estate-level wet mill infrastructure (particularly in Antigua and Atitlán) enables precise, consistent processing. The East African raised-bed drying model has been adopted by quality-focused estates and cooperatives alongside traditional patio drying.

**Honey and natural processing** are growing as specialty buyers and Anacafé promote differentiated lots. The Cup of Excellence now includes naturally processed lots, and several Huehuetenango and Acatenango producers have invested in natural processing infrastructure.

## Varieties and Genetic Diversity

Guatemala's variety landscape is one of the most diverse in Central America:

- **Bourbon**: The dominant traditional variety; associated with the finest Antigua and Huehuetenango lots; lower yield than Caturra but prized for cup quality; Guatemala retains significant Bourbon hectarage relative to most other Central American countries
- **Typica**: Historic variety, older plantings; declining; associated with some traditional estates
- **Caturra**: Dwarf Bourbon mutation; high yield; widespread among smallholders and medium estates; good cup quality
- **Catuai** (Yellow and Red): Mundo Novo × Caturra; high yield; very common in commercial-scale production
- **Pache**: A dwarf Typica mutation native to Guatemala; compact growth; associated with Atitlán and Fraijanes; produces a clean, nuanced cup
- **Gesha (Geisha)**: Growing premium lot production in Huehuetenango and Acatenango high-altitude farms; Guatemalan Gesha lots at auction have attracted significant international attention
- **Maracaturra**: Maragogipe × Caturra; present on some Huehuetenango farms; large bean; distinctive mild cup

Guatemala is notably one of the few Central American countries that has maintained significant Bourbon plantings rather than replacing the variety entirely with higher-yielding Caturra hybrids — a decision that reflects the premium cup quality premium that the specialty market attaches to Bourbon character.

## Specialty Coffee

Guatemala's specialty identity is built on regional diversity, estate traceability, and the dominance of Bourbon and Typica varieties in the most celebrated producing areas. The defining specialty achievements are:

- **Cup of Excellence** (since 2001): Guatemala was one of the earliest CoE programme countries in Central America. Annual CoE auctions have identified world-class lots primarily from Huehuetenango, Acatenango, and Atitlán — the high-altitude zones producing the most complex and clean profiles. Guatemalan CoE lots routinely score 87–93 SCA, and auction prices for top lots have reached US$50+ per pound in competitive years.
- **Finca El Injerto** (Huehuetenango): One of the most awarded estates in Central America, with multiple CoE first-place wins and multiple records for highest auction price. El Injerto's Bourbon and Peaberry lots are reference points for Guatemalan specialty quality globally.
- **ACODIHUE cooperative** (Huehuetenango): A large multi-community Maya cooperative providing direct-trade access and organic certification for smallholder members; a flagship of smallholder specialty development in Guatemala.
- **Antigua**: The original Guatemalan specialty identity; a consistent and reliable benchmark for Central American washed Arabica quality.

## Coffee Competitions

### Cup of Excellence — Guatemala

Guatemala's Cup of Excellence began in 2001, making it one of the programme's earliest participants. Annual competitions attract 300–500+ entries nationally; a National Jury phase reduces these to semifinalists, then finalists scoring 87+ SCA points for the International Jury phase. Guatemala CoE has produced some of the highest auction prices in the programme's history, with Huehuetenango and Acatenango lots commanding international specialty buyer bidding wars. The programme is administered by Anacafé in partnership with the Alliance for Coffee Excellence.

### National Barista and Competition Circuit

Guatemala hosts national barista competitions under Anacafé's coordination, selecting representatives for the World Barista Championship and related SCA events. Guatemalan baristas have competed at WBC using Guatemalan Bourbon, Gesha, and specialty washed lots. The domestic barista scene, centred on Guatemala City, is among the more developed in Central America, supported by the country's strong café culture and estate-based specialty coffee visibility.

## Key Facts

- Eight Anacafé-designated origin regions: Antigua, Huehuetenango, Cobán, Atitlán, San Marcos, Fraijanes, New Oriente, Acatenango
- Annual production: ~3.5–4.5 million sixty-kilogram bags; 100% Arabica; ~2–3% of global supply
- ~120,000 producing families; mix of large estates and Maya smallholders
- Anacafé (est. 1960): one of Latin America's most active national coffee organisations
- Dominant varieties: Bourbon, Caturra, Catuai, Typica; Pache (Guatemala-native dwarf); Gesha in premium lots
- Washed processing standard; estate wet mill infrastructure well-developed
- Coffee introduced ~18th century; German-estate dominance 1860s–1944; WWII expropriation transformed ownership structure
- Cup of Excellence since 2001; Finca El Injerto: multiple CoE first-place wins
- Volcán Tajumulco (4,220 m): highest point in Central America; in San Marcos coffee region
