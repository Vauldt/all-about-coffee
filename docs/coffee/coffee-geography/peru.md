## Overview

Peru is South America's third-largest coffee producer and one of the world's leading exporters of certified organic coffee, with cultivation concentrated in the high-altitude cloud forest zones of the eastern Andes — the *ceja de selva* — across Cajamarca, Amazonas, San Martín, and Junín. Peruvian coffee is grown almost entirely by smallholder farmers on plots of one to three hectares, organised through a large cooperative sector that serves as the primary vehicle for international market access, organic certification, and fair-trade distribution. The national cup profile is broadly clean and sweet, with soft acidity, chocolate, and stone fruit notes, though high-altitude Cajamarca and Amazonas lots increasingly demonstrate floral complexity that places them among the continent's most interesting specialty offerings.

---

## Country Overview

Peru occupies the western seaboard and Andean cordillera of central South America, bordered by Ecuador and Colombia to the north, Brazil and Bolivia to the east, and Chile to the south, covering 1.28 million km². The landscape divides into three distinct zones: the arid Pacific coastal plain (*costa*), the high Andes mountain ranges (*sierra*), and the vast Amazonian lowlands (*selva*). Coffee is grown in the *selva alta* — the transitional cloud forest on the eastern slopes of the Andes — at altitudes ranging from around 900 to 2,100 metres.

The population of approximately 33 million is concentrated in Lima on the coast and in the Andean highland cities. Spanish is the official language; Quechua and Aymara are also recognised as co-official languages in highland regions. Indigenous Andean and Amazonian communities maintain significant presence in the coffee-growing departments, and many cooperative members speak Quechua as a primary language.

## The Coffee Industry

Coffee is Peru's primary agricultural export and one of its most economically significant crop sectors, with approximately 220,000 smallholder farming families dependent on coffee cultivation across roughly 400,000 hectares of productive area. The Junta Nacional del Café (JNC) is the principal industry body, coordinating between the state, cooperative sector, and international buyers. The Ministry of Agrarian Development and Irrigation (MIDAGRI) provides technical support and manages national agrarian development programmes including pest and disease response.

The cooperative system is the dominant organisational structure in Peruvian coffee. Major cooperatives include CENFROCAFÉ (Cajamarca), COCLA (Cusco and Puno), CAC La Florida (Junín), and Aproeco (Amazonas). These organisations manage wet milling, quality control, export logistics, and certification auditing on behalf of their smallholder members. Peru is consistently among the world's top five exporters of certified organic coffee and one of the largest fair-trade coffee suppliers globally.

Germany, the United States, Belgium, and Sweden are the principal export destinations. The European market's demand for certified organic and fair-trade products has historically shaped Peru's export identity, though the growth of the specialty coffee market has created a parallel channel for higher-scoring, traceable single-origin lots.

## History of Coffee in Peru

Coffee was introduced to Peru by Jesuit missionaries in the mid-18th century, initially cultivated in highland garden plots around Huánuco and the Chanchamayo Valley in Junín. Commercial cultivation expanded in the late 19th century as British and German interests developed hacienda estates in the Chanchamayo Valley and Puno, attracted by the fertile selva alta soils and proximity to Lima via the Central Highway.

The hacienda system dominated production through the early 20th century. General Juan Velasco Alvarado's Agrarian Reform of 1969 dismantled the hacienda structure and redistributed land to smallholder farming families, creating the fragmented smallholder landscape that defines Peruvian coffee production today. The cooperative movement grew through the 1970s and 1980s as smallholders organised collectively to access processing infrastructure and export markets.

Shining Path and MRTA insurgency activity severely disrupted the northern coffee-growing departments of Cajamarca and Amazonas during the 1980s and early 1990s, displacing farmers and collapsing infrastructure investment. The pacification of the 1990s allowed recovery and export growth, and the decade saw rapid expansion of organic certification as international buyers sought compliant supply.

The 2012–2013 *Roya* (coffee leaf rust, *Hemileia vastatrix*) epidemic was the most severe agricultural crisis in modern Peruvian coffee history. An estimated 290,000 hectares — more than 70 per cent of the productive area — were affected, and national production fell sharply. International aid and emergency FAO programmes funded replanting and Catimor distribution. Recovery took until 2017–2018, and the epidemic accelerated the adoption of rust-resistant varieties across all growing regions.

## Domestic Production

Peru produces approximately 210,000–230,000 metric tonnes of green coffee per year in strong years, fluctuating with climatic variation and ongoing leaf rust pressure. All production is Arabica; Robusta cultivation is essentially absent. The farming system is characterised by extreme smallholder fragmentation: average farm size is one to three hectares, managed by individual families with minimal mechanisation.

Harvest timing varies by altitude and latitude:

| Region | Main Harvest |
| :--- | :--- |
| Cajamarca (north) | May–September |
| Amazonas | April–August |
| San Martín | April–August |
| Junín | April–July |
| Puno / Cusco (south) | June–October |

Processing is almost universally washed (wet process), carried out at cooperative wet mills or small estate beneficios. Parchment coffee is dried on raised beds or cement patios before being transported to centralised dry mills for hulling, grading, and export preparation. Natural and honey processing are beginning to appear in specialty-oriented cooperative lots but remain a very small fraction of overall production.

## Coffee-Growing Regions

| Region | Altitude | Character |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Cajamarca | 1,200–2,100 m | Peru's most celebrated specialty zone; floral, bright, high-altitude complexity |
| Amazonas | 1,400–2,100 m | Emerging specialty profile; floral, complex, underrecognised |
| San Martín | 900–1,500 m | Largest volume region; balanced, sweet, accessible commercial profile |
| Junín | 1,000–1,800 m | Full-bodied; chocolate and stone fruit; significant cooperative activity |
| Puno | 1,400–2,000 m | Southern highland; softer, round profile; smaller volume |
| Cusco | 1,000–1,700 m | Mild, balanced; historical growing region in La Convención Valley |

**Cajamarca** — particularly the districts of Jaén and San Ignacio bordering Ecuador — is Peru's most internationally recognised specialty zone. Altitudes reaching 2,100 metres, slow cherry maturation, and well-drained Andean soils produce concentrated sugars and aromatic complexity. **Amazonas**, particularly Rodríguez de Mendoza, is receiving increasing specialty buyer attention for its floral, high-altitude lots. **San Martín** accounts for the largest share of national production volume, with a reliable commercial profile that underpins organic and fair-trade export contracts.

## Varieties and Genetic Diversity

Typica dominates Peruvian production across all regions — a legacy of the colonial-era introductions that spread Typica through South America. The variety's susceptibility to *Roya* and low productivity are recognised weaknesses, but its cup quality, particularly at high altitude, remains valued in the specialty market. Bourbon and Caturra are present across the cooperative-organised regions and perform well in specialty lots from Cajamarca and Amazonas.

Catimor (and related Timor Hybrid derivatives) was widely distributed following the 2012–2013 leaf rust epidemic and is now the dominant variety in many replanted areas. It offers robust rust resistance and reasonable productivity, but its cup profile is considered inferior at lower altitudes, producing astringency and robusta-character defects. Gesha has been introduced on a small number of specialty estates in Cajamarca with promising early auction results.

## Specialty Coffee

Peru's specialty identity has developed in two parallel streams. The first is the volume-driven certified organic and fair-trade market, where cooperatives have been effective in accessing European premium channels since the late 1990s. The second is the emerging high-quality specialty segment, driven by Cup of Excellence competition results and the growing international recognition of Cajamarca and Amazonas as high-altitude origin zones.

Key cooperative organisations in the specialty sector include CENFROCAFÉ and Norandino (both Cajamarca/Piura) and CAC La Florida (Junín). These cooperatives have invested in raised-bed drying, quality training, and cupping laboratory infrastructure to access specialty premiums above certified-organic floor prices.

The principal challenges for Peruvian specialty development are inconsistent post-harvest infrastructure at farm level, the dominance of Catimor in replanted areas, and the relatively limited domestic specialty coffee culture that would otherwise support quality feedback loops.

## Coffee Competitions

**Cup of Excellence (CoE):** Peru joined the Cup of Excellence programme in 2017, with annual national competitions held since then. The programme has been the most significant vehicle for identifying and publicising Peru's highest-quality lots, with Cajamarca, Amazonas, and Junín producers consistently placing in the top ranks.

**National competitions:** The Junta Nacional del Café organises national quality competitions alongside cooperative-level internal grading programmes. The Peru Barista Championship, affiliated with the World Coffee Championships through the Specialty Coffee Association, selects national representatives for international competition.

**International participation:** Peruvian baristas have competed at the World Barista Championship, and Peruvian cooperatives have participated in the SCA's Sustainability Award programmes. Peru has received Fairtrade and Rainforest Alliance certifications at scale, which involve third-party auditing rather than competition.

## Key Facts

- South America's third-largest coffee producer; ~210,000–230,000 MT/yr
- ~220,000 smallholder farming families; average plot 1–3 ha; entirely Arabica
- Growing regions: Cajamarca, Amazonas, San Martín, Junín, Puno, Cusco
- Altitude range: 900–2,100 m; best specialty quality above 1,400 m
- Dominant varieties: Typica, Bourbon, Caturra; Catimor post-*Roya* replanting
- One of the world's largest exporters of certified organic coffee
- Processing: almost universally washed; cooperative wet mills dominant
- 2012–2013 *Roya* epidemic affected ~70% of productive area; recovery by 2017–2018
- Cup of Excellence programme began 2017
- Principal export markets: Germany, USA, Belgium, Sweden
