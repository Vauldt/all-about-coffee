## Overview

Coffee production encompasses everything that occurs between the seed in the ground and the green coffee bag arriving at the roastery — cultivation, harvesting, processing, and export. Production knowledge provides essential context for quality decisions, supply chain conversations, and sustainability assessments across the coffee trade.

## The Coffee Plant

Coffee is an evergreen shrub or small tree of the genus *Coffea*. *Coffea arabica* is the species responsible for most specialty coffee. Key plant biology:

**Growth cycle:** A coffee plant takes approximately three to five years from seed or seedling to first commercial production. Once established, a healthy plant can produce for 20–30 years. A single Arabica plant yields approximately 1–2 kg of green coffee per year.

**Flowering:** Coffee flowers are small, white, jasmine-scented blossoms that appear after a triggering event — typically rain after a dry period. All flowers on a tree bloom simultaneously within a few days. Fruit development follows.

**Cherry maturation:** After flowering, the cherry takes six to eight months to ripen (Arabica at altitude). Slow maturation at cool temperatures develops the complex organic acids and sugars that define specialty coffee flavour.

**Continuous vs. annual harvest:** In regions with distinct wet and dry seasons, harvest occurs once per year. In regions near the equator with bimodal rainfall (Colombia, parts of Central America), two harvests per year are possible.

## Agricultural Systems

**Smallholder farming:** The dominant model in Africa and much of Central America. Typically 0.5–5 hectare plots, often intercropped with food crops and shade trees. Labour-intensive and family-managed. Quality can be exceptional but consistency across seasons requires cooperative infrastructure.

**Cooperative model:** Smallholders deliver cherry to a centralised washing station or processing facility owned by a cooperative. The cooperative aggregates production, manages processing, and markets the combined output. This model underlies most Ethiopian, Kenyan, and Rwandan specialty coffee.

**Estate/plantation farming:** Larger single-ownership farms with their own processing facilities. More consistent between seasons and easier to achieve traceability. Common in Brazil, Central America, and Indonesia.

**Mechanised farming:** Brazil's large-scale, flat-terrain farms use mechanical harvesters (strip harvesting or mechanical shakers). Viable only in flat terrain with uniform ripening; reduces labour costs significantly at lower selectivity.

## Harvesting Methods

**Selective (hand) picking:** Pickers harvest only fully ripe red (or yellow) cherries, leaving unripe and overripe fruit on the tree. The most labour-intensive and expensive method; produces the highest quality raw material. Multiple passes through the same trees are required as cherries ripen at different rates. Standard in most specialty-oriented production.

**Strip picking (hand):** All cherries on a branch are stripped at once, regardless of ripeness. Faster and cheaper; results in a mix of ripe, under-ripe, and overripe cherries that requires sorting. Common in Brazil.

**Mechanical harvesting:** Machines shake or beat trees to dislodge cherries. Used in Brazil and some large estates. Very efficient but lowest selectivity.

**Float separation:** Regardless of picking method, a water float is used to separate by density. Ripe, dense cherries sink; under-ripe, overripe, and damaged cherries float and are removed.

## Supply Chain: From Farm to Roastery

**Farm → Wet mill / Washing station:** Cherry is delivered to the processing facility within hours of picking (delay increases fermentation risk). Processing (washed, natural, honey) occurs here. See [Processing Methods](../coffee-processing/processing-methods.md).

**Wet mill → Dry mill / Export mill:** After processing, parchment coffee is transported to a dry milling facility where it is hulled (parchment removed), sorted by screen size and density, inspected for defects, and packed into export bags.

**Export:** Coffee is typically exported in 60 kg jute bags or, increasingly, in GrainPro or vacuum-sealed bags inside jute for better moisture and aroma protection.

**Importer / exporter:** Exporters at origin (often government-licensed) and importers at destination manage the logistics and documentation. Specialty importers often develop direct relationships with producers and offer more traceable lots.

**Roastery:** The roastery receives green coffee and roasts it to specification.

**Direct trade:** Some roasters establish direct relationships with producers, bypassing the traditional importer layer. This enables better traceability, farmer premiums above commodity prices, and more collaborative quality development.

## Varieties and Their Production Characteristics

**Typica:** One of the oldest Arabica varieties; tall, low-yield, susceptible to disease. Complex cup profile; not commercially viable at scale without premium pricing.

**Bourbon:** Slightly higher yield than Typica; excellent cup quality; susceptible to disease. Red, yellow, and orange Bourbon varieties exist.

**Caturra:** Natural mutation of Bourbon; compact, higher-yield, easier to manage at altitude. Common in Colombia and Central America.

**Castillo:** Disease-resistant hybrid bred in Colombia. Higher yield and rust resistance; cup quality has improved with newer generations.

**SL-28 and SL-34 (Kenya):** Scott Laboratories selections from the 1930s–40s. Outstanding cup quality; disease-susceptible; require high-input management.

**Gesha/Geisha:** Originally from the Gesha region of Ethiopia; grown in Panama and increasingly elsewhere. Extremely low yield; exceptional floral and jasmine cup character; commands the highest auction prices.

**Ruiru 11 / Batian (Kenya):** Disease-resistant Kenyan hybrids; higher yield and rust resistance than SL varieties; considered somewhat less complex but increasingly important as Kenya faces climate and disease pressure.

## Climate Change and Coffee Production

Climate change is the most significant structural threat to Arabica coffee production:

**Temperature increase:** Arabica requires a narrow temperature range (18–24 °C). A 2 °C rise shifts suitable altitude bands upward by approximately 200–300 m, potentially excluding lower-altitude growing areas.

**Rainfall variability:** Irregular rainfall disrupts flowering trigger mechanisms and creates uncertainty in harvest timing and cherry development.

**Pest and disease pressure:** Coffee Leaf Rust (*Hemileia vastatrix*) devastated Central American production in the 2012–13 outbreak. Rising temperatures expand the altitude range of the fungus, increasing disease pressure on high-altitude farms previously insulated by cold.

**Adaptation strategies:** Diversification into disease-resistant varieties, shade-grown farming (moderates temperature extremes), altitude migration, and agroforestry are all being pursued.

## Certifications and Standards

**Fairtrade:** Sets minimum price floors and social standards; primarily a commodity pricing mechanism.

**Rainforest Alliance / UTZ (merged):** Focuses on environmental and social farming standards.

**Organic certification:** Limits synthetic pesticide and fertiliser use; relevant to environmental footprint but not a direct indicator of cup quality.

**Direct trade:** Not a formal certification; describes a relationship rather than a standard.

**Cup of Excellence (CoE):** Competitive auction programme that identifies and rewards exceptional quality lots. CoE coffees are rigorously cupped by an international panel and sold at auction — typically at significant price premiums that flow to the producer.

## Key Facts

- A single Arabica plant yields approximately 1–2 kg of green coffee per year; first commercial production takes three to five years from planting
- Cherry maturation takes six to eight months from flowering; slow high-altitude maturation builds flavour complexity
- The cooperative model is the dominant structure for smallholder specialty coffee in East Africa
- Selective hand picking — harvesting only ripe cherries across multiple passes — produces the highest quality raw material
- Arabica optimal growing temperature is 18–24 °C; a 2 °C rise shifts viable altitude bands 200–300 m upward
- Coffee Leaf Rust (*Hemileia vastatrix*) is the most significant disease threat to Arabica production globally
