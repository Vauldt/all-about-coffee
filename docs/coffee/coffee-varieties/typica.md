## Overview

Typica is one of the two foundational Arabica variety groups from which the vast majority of cultivated coffee descends, the other being Bourbon. Originating from the Yemeni port city of Mocha and the early Arabian coffee trade, Typica was the first Arabica variety to be distributed globally by Dutch and French colonial networks in the 17th and 18th centuries, reaching India, Indonesia, the Caribbean, and the Americas. Most of the named cultivars grown commercially today — Caturra, Catuai, Mundo Novo, Kent, Sumatra Typica, Blue Mountain, Maragogipe, and many others — trace their genetics directly to Typica. The variety produces a refined, clean cup with high acidity and moderate body, but its low yield and susceptibility to disease have driven widespread replacement by higher-yielding descendants.

## Origins and Dispersal

Wild Arabica coffee originates in the forests of southwestern Ethiopia, but the variety group known as Typica derives from the narrow genetic bottleneck of plants taken from Ethiopia to Yemen, where coffee was first cultivated commercially. From Yemen, the Dutch East India Company transported Arabica seedlings to their botanical garden in Amsterdam in 1706, and subsequently to their colonial plantations in Malabar (India) and Java (Indonesia). A single plant from the Amsterdam collection was gifted to French King Louis XIV and housed at the Jardin des Plantes in Paris; a cutting from this plant was transported to Martinique by Gabriel de Clieu in 1723. From Martinique, Typica spread through the French Caribbean and into Central and South America.

The result of this dispersal pattern is that virtually all Latin American Arabica — and much of the world's commercial Arabica — descends from an extremely small founding population, representing a severe reduction in the genetic diversity present in Ethiopia's wild coffee forests.

## Botanical Characteristics

| Attribute | Description |
| :--- | :--- |
| Plant height | Tall — 3.5–4.5 m; requires shade or pruning in commercial settings |
| Branch angle | Oblique, with a conical overall shape |
| Leaf tips | Bronze to copper when young; distinctive identifier |
| Bean shape | Elongated, slightly pointed at each end |
| Cherry colour | Deep red at full ripeness |
| Yield | Low relative to modern cultivars |
| Altitude | Performs best at 1,200–1,800 m |

The bronze leaf tips are one of the most reliable visual identifiers of Typica and Typica-derived varieties in the field.

## Cup Profile

Typica is regarded as the reference standard against which other Arabica varieties are often measured. When grown at appropriate altitude with good agricultural practice, it produces:

- **Aroma:** Clean, sweet, with floral and fruit notes
- **Acidity:** Bright, well-defined, citric and malic
- **Body:** Medium — less than Bourbon, more than most light-roast Kenyan varieties
- **Flavour:** Stone fruit, mild citrus, caramel sweetness; transparent expression of terroir
- **Aftertaste:** Clean and pleasant

The cup's clarity and sweetness are valued in specialty contexts. Typica's cup profile is considered elegant rather than intense — it shows terroir and processing method with particular transparency.

## Disease and Pest Susceptibility

Typica's primary commercial weakness is its susceptibility to the two most economically damaging coffee diseases:

- **Coffee leaf rust** (*Hemileia vastatrix*): Typica has no natural resistance. The 1869 outbreak in Ceylon (Sri Lanka) wiped out the island's entire Typica-based coffee industry, converting it to tea production. Recurring outbreaks across Central America have devastated Typica-growing regions throughout the 20th and 21st centuries.
- **Coffee berry disease** (CBD, *Colletotrichum kahawae*): Particularly damaging in East Africa, where Typica and its derivatives are widely grown.

These vulnerabilities have driven the breeding of resistant cultivars, including Catimor (Timor Hybrid × Caturra), Sarchimor, and the Kenyan SL series, all of which sacrifice some cup quality for disease tolerance.

## Key Descendants

Typica's global dispersal seeded an enormous family of named cultivars:

| Cultivar | Origin | Notes |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Caturra | Brazil, natural mutation | Dwarf Bourbon (not Typica direct), but widely treated as part of the family |
| Maragogipe | Brazil, natural mutation | Giant-bean Typica mutation; large, porous beans |
| Sumatra Typica | Indonesia | The foundational Sumatran variety; gives Mandheling/Lintong character |
| Jamaica Blue Mountain | Jamaica | Selected Typica population; protected GI designation |
| Kona | Hawaii | Typica population selected in Hawaii; GI-protected |
| Kent | India | Early rust-tolerant Typica selection; now superseded |
| San Ramon | Costa Rica | Dwarf Typica mutation |

## Commercial Status

Pure Typica is rare in commercial production today. Its low yield — roughly half that of Caturra or Catuai under equivalent conditions — makes it economically unviable for most smallholder farmers without premium pricing. It survives in:

- **Jamaica and Hawaii**, where GI designations command sufficient premium to justify its low productivity
- **Indonesia**, where Sumatra Typica remains foundational to the island's coffee character
- **Peru and some Central American microfarms**, where specialty buyers pay a premium for the variety's cup quality
- **Heritage and research collections** maintained by institutions such as CATIE (Costa Rica) and World Coffee Research

## Key Facts

- One of two founding Arabica variety groups, alongside Bourbon
- All Latin American Arabica descends from a single Martinique plant sourced from the Paris Jardin des Plantes (1723)
- Identified in the field by bronze/copper leaf tips and conical plant shape
- Low yield and high disease susceptibility have driven commercial replacement by derivatives
- Pure Typica survives commercially in Jamaica (Blue Mountain), Hawaii (Kona), and Indonesia (Sumatra Typica)
- Produces a clean, transparent cup that expresses terroir with exceptional clarity
