# Zimbabwe and Its Coffee Culture

Zimbabwe sits in southern Africa, landlocked and elevated — a geography that quietly makes it one of the continent's more intriguing, if lesser-known, [specialty coffee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Specialty_coffee) origins. Its coffee story is one of colonial introduction, dramatic collapse, and a slow, determined revival.

---

## A Brief History of Coffee in Zimbabwe

Coffee arrived in Zimbabwe through [European colonisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colonisation_of_Africa) around the 1850s, with accounts of British explorer [David Livingstone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Livingstone) receiving coffee beans from native tribes during his mid-19th century travels through the region. Plantations began to expand, but in the 1920s a widespread plant disease wiped out nearly all of the country's coffee stock, effectively ending production for around four decades.

The industry revived commercially in the [1960s](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Zimbabwe), when Zimbabwe (then Rhodesia) established strict classification and grading standards that made its beans export-worthy. By the late 1980s, production peaked at approximately **16,000 metric tonnes annually** — coffee had become the country's fifth-largest export, coveted in London and New York for its rich, chocolatey flavour and vibrant acidity.

The turning point came with [Robert Mugabe's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Mugabe) land reform programme from 2000 onwards, which forcibly reclaimed thousands of farms. The Zimbabwean coffee industry was effectively destroyed overnight, with production collapsing by more than 90%.

## Growing Regions

Almost all of Zimbabwe's coffee is grown along the eastern border, in an area collectively known as the **Eastern Highlands**. The key production zones are:

- **[Chipinge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chipinge)** — Located in [Manicaland Province](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manicaland_Province), near the Mozambican border. Chipinge is the most important coffee-growing district in the country, responsible for the vast majority of Zimbabwe's specialty output. It boasts altitudes equivalent to around 1,600 metres above sea level, producing coffees with a vibrant, winey acidity comparable to the best East African origins.
- **[Vumba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vumba_Mountains)** — A mountainous area known for its mist-laden climate, producing nuanced, aromatic lots.
- **[Honde Valley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Honde_Valley)** — Running along the Mozambican border within Manicaland and Mashonaland provinces; a key zone for both estate and smallholder production.

Together, Vumba and Chipinge account for roughly **75% of the country's specialty Arabica** production.

## Varietals and Processing

The dominant [coffee varietal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_coffee_varieties) in Zimbabwe is **[Catimor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catimor)** — a hybrid of [Caturra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caturra_coffee) and [Timor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timor_coffee), originally developed in Portugal. Catimor is favoured for its disease resistance, short maturity period, and compact size, which supports higher yields per plant. It is grown on more than 90% of farms in Chipinge. Other varietals present in the country include **[SL28](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SL28)** and **[Bourbon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bourbon_coffee)**.

Most Zimbabwean coffee is [wet processed (washed)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coffee_processing#Wet_processing), which tends to produce clean, bright cups that express the region's characteristic acidity clearly.

---

## Flavour Profile

Zimbabwean coffee is typically described as:

- **Medium-bodied** with a **lively, winey acidity**
- Tasting notes of **red berries, currants, cranberries, and dark chocolate**
- Highly aromatic with **complex fruitiness**
- A **zesty brightness** that resembles the best Kenyan and Ethiopian lots

On the African continent, Chipinge coffee is widely considered to be exceeded in quality only by [Kenyan coffee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kenyan_coffee).

## Domestic Coffee Culture

Zimbabwe is, at its heart, a **tea-drinking nation**. Coffee consumption domestically has historically been associated with the wealthier classes, making it less embedded in everyday culture than in [Ethiopia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethiopian_coffee_ceremony) or even neighbouring [Kenya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coffee_production_in_Kenya). However, this is shifting, with a growing urban café culture — particularly in [Harare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harare) and [Bulawayo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bulawayo) — beginning to embrace specialty coffee.

The local coffee sector is increasingly driven by small, family-run farms, whose stories are inseparable from the beans they produce. By 2016, only **400 smallholder farmers** remained active. That number has grown since, driven in part by external programmes supporting the revival.

---

## The Revival

Since 2017, organisations like [TechnoServe](https://www.technoserve.org) and [Nespresso's Reviving Origins programme](https://www.nespresso.com/au/en/reviving-origins) have worked together to rebuild Zimbabwe's coffee-growing capacity. In 2019, Nespresso launched a single-origin coffee called **Tamuka Mu Zimbabwe** ("Awakening of Zimbabwe"), now available in 18 countries — a symbolic and commercial marker of the industry's resurgence.

Estates like [Zimunda](https://www.zimunda.com/coffee) have invested heavily in regenerating original varietal plantings, introducing sustainable practices such as aquaponics-based irrigation and [agroforestry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agroforestry). With global coffee consumption projected to grow at approximately **4.22% annually** between 2024 and 2028, Zimbabwe is actively positioning itself as a serious contender in the [specialty coffee](https://sca.coffee) market.
