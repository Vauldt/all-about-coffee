# Ethiopia

Ethiopia is widely regarded as the birthplace of *Coffea arabica* and occupies a singular position in coffee history: it is the only country where arabica coffee grows wild in native forest ecosystems, and it supports a domestic coffee culture as deeply ingrained as any on earth. For the specialty industry, Ethiopia is not merely an origin — it is a reference point for what arabica coffee can express when genetic diversity, altitude, and traditional cultivation converge.

---

## Country Overview

Ethiopia is a landlocked country in the Horn of Africa, covering approximately 1.1 million km², and is bordered by Eritrea and Djibouti to the north and east, Somalia to the east, Kenya to the south, and Sudan and South Sudan to the west. It is one of the most populous countries in Africa, with a population of approximately 138 million people as of 2026, encompassing more than 80 distinct ethnic groups and languages.

The country's topography is dominated by the **Ethiopian Highlands**, which form the largest continuous mountain mass in Africa, rising above 4,000 m in places. Bisecting this plateau is the **Great Rift Valley**, which runs from the northeast to the southwest and creates a dramatic landscape of escarpments, lakes, and lowland plains. This varied terrain produces an equally varied range of microclimates — the same physical geography that makes Ethiopia such a botanically rich environment for coffee cultivation.

Ethiopia operates under a federal administrative structure divided into regional states, zones, woredas (districts), and kebeles (municipalities). The capital and largest city is **Addis Ababa**.

## Ethiopia and Coffee

### The Birthplace of Arabica

*Coffea arabica* is native to the montane forests of southwestern and southeastern Ethiopia, particularly in the historical region of **Kaffa** (from which the word *coffee* is often said to derive, though the etymology is disputed). It was here, in the wild forest understorey at elevations of 1,500–2,400 m, that arabica evolved as a species — and wild arabica trees still grow across these forests today. Ethiopia's genetic reservoir of arabica material is unmatched anywhere in the world: estimates suggest between 6,000 and 10,000 distinct varieties exist within the country, the vast majority uncatalogued and collectively referred to as **heirloom** or **landrace** varieties.

This genetic depth is the foundation of Ethiopian coffee's remarkable diversity in the cup. Ethiopian coffees are capable of expressing a wider range of sensory profiles than those of any other producing country, from the delicate jasmine and bergamot of a high-altitude Yirgacheffe to the dense blueberry and dark chocolate of a natural-processed Guji.

### The Kaldi Legend

The most widely repeated origin story for coffee's discovery involves **Kaldi**, an Ethiopian goat herder working somewhere in the highlands, traditionally in the 9th century. According to the legend, Kaldi observed his goats behaving with unusual energy — dancing, even — after eating the bright red berries of an unfamiliar shrub. He sampled the berries himself and experienced the same stimulating effect. The discovery eventually reached local monks, who brewed a beverage from the berries to help sustain themselves through long nights of prayer.

The Kaldi story is almost certainly apocryphal — it appears in no written source earlier than the 17th century — but it endures as a cultural shorthand for Ethiopia's foundational role in coffee history. It is worth noting that the **Oromo people** of the Guji region carry a parallel origin narrative of their own: in Oromo tradition, the sky god *Waaqa* caused a coffee plant to grow from tears shed over the wrongful death of a loyal servant, and from at least the 10th century, Oromo communities are recorded as consuming coffee — grinding the beans with animal fat into portable energy balls for warriors on long journeys.

### Production Systems

Ethiopia's coffee is produced through three traditional systems, which co-exist across different regions:

| System | Description |
|---|---|
| **Forest coffee** | Grows entirely wild in native highland forest under canopy shade. Organic by default (though rarely certified). Cherries are collected by local communities from unmanaged or semi-managed wild trees. Represents Ethiopia's most ancient and genetically diverse coffee. |
| **Garden coffee** | Grown on small household plots of under one hectare, often intercropped with food crops, vegetables, and shade trees. Measured by tree count rather than land area. Uses ancient landrace varieties and is generally organic. The most common system in Ethiopia. |
| **Estate / plantation coffee** | A small percentage of total production. Large managed farms with more controlled inputs. Smallholders without their own processing facilities typically deliver cherries to centralised **washing stations** (wet mills) operated by cooperatives or private exporters, or process naturally at home. |

### Varieties and Genetic Diversity

The extraordinary number of arabica types found in Ethiopia resists simple classification. Commercial bags typically use umbrella terms:

- **Heirloom** — traditionally cultivated varieties passed down through generations (implies human selection).
- **Landrace** — varieties naturally adapted to a specific environment through natural selection with minimal human intervention; genetically broader.

In practice, the two terms are often used interchangeably on packaging and green coffee documentation. The **Jimma Agricultural Research Center (JARC)**, established in 1967, has done the most systematic work on Ethiopian variety identification, releasing 42 named cultivars suited to different regions — including **Wolisho** and **Dega**, named after indigenous trees — as well as disease-resistant lines for farmers.

Notable identified varieties include:

- **Gesha (Geisha)** — originated in the Gesha forest of southwestern Ethiopia; later cultivated at Lyamungu Research Station in Tanzania and then brought to Central America, where it rose to global prominence after the Peterson family's 2005 Best of Panama win. Now among the most expensive coffees in the world.
- **Kurume and Wolisho** — localised Gedeo/Yirgacheffe landraces particularly associated with that region's characteristic florality and brightness.

## Coffee Regions

Ethiopia's principal growing areas are concentrated in the southern highlands, the southeastern highlands, and the west. The **Ethiopia Commodity Exchange (ECX)**, established in 2008, classifies green coffee by geographically defined trade areas rather than precise administrative boundaries — meaning origin labels on bags reflect market and quality classifications, not always the exact political district of origin. Major ECX-recognised origins include:

| Origin | Location | Elevation | Processing tendency | Cup character |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Yirgacheffe** | Gedeo Zone, SNNPR | 1,600–2,400 m | Predominantly washed | Bright, floral, tea-like, complex aromatics, citrus |
| **Sidama (Sidamo)** | Sidama Region + broader highlands | 1,550–2,200 m | Both washed and natural | Floral, citrus, crisp acidity, rich body |
| **Guji** | Guji Zone, Oromia | 1,800–2,350 m | Natural and washed | Fruit-forward, berries, citrus, balanced acidity |
| **Harrar (Harar)** | Harrar/East Oromia | 1,400–2,000 m | Natural (dry process) | Winey, berry, mocha, full body |
| **Limu** | Jimma Zone, Oromia | 1,100–1,900 m | Predominantly washed | Sweet, balanced, sometimes spicy |
| **Djimma (Jimma)** | Jimma Zone, Oromia | 1,300–2,100 m | Both | Variable; often used in commercial blends |

For detailed notes on the southern origins, see Ethiopian Southern Coffee Growing Regions.

### Coffee Grading

Ethiopia uses a numerical grading system from **Grade 1 (highest)** to **Grade 9 (lowest)**. Grades 1 and 2 qualify as specialty. Grading is based on a combination of physical bean attributes (defect count, screen size, moisture), altitude, and cup quality assessed through cupping. Natural and washed coffees are assessed under slightly different criteria, with natural coffees subject to more scrutiny given the higher risk of defects in dry processing.

## The Coffee Economy

Coffee is Ethiopia's most important export commodity and a cornerstone of the national economy. Key figures:

- Ethiopia produces approximately **8.2 million 60 kg bags** of 100% arabica per year, representing roughly **3–4% of global supply**.
- Coffee accounts for over **one-third of Ethiopia's export revenue**; some sources cite approximately 24% of total export income depending on the year and measurement.
- In the first nine months of the 2024–25 Ethiopian fiscal year, Ethiopia exported **299,607 tonnes** of coffee, generating **US$1.508 billion** in revenue — a record. Major export destinations included Saudi Arabia, Germany, and the United States ([Global Coffee Report](https://www.gcrmag.com/ethiopia-records-new-coffee-exportation-record/)).
- Approximately **25% of the Ethiopian population** depends on the coffee value chain for their livelihoods.

The ECX introduced centralised trading infrastructure and traceability systems, with significant revisions in 2017 allowing full lot-level traceability and enabling farmers to apply directly for export licences. It is illegal under Ethiopian law to sell export-quality coffee on the domestic market.

## Domestic Coffee Culture

Unlike most coffee-exporting countries, Ethiopia consumes a substantial share of its own production — approximately **40–50%** of total output is estimated to remain in-country. Coffee is not simply an economic crop in Ethiopia; it is a social institution.

### The Coffee Ceremony (*Buna Tetu*)

The Ethiopian coffee ceremony is the most visible expression of coffee's cultural role. The Amharic phrase *buna tetu* translates literally as "come drink coffee with us" — a phrase of hospitality, not ceremony, which reflects how naturally the ritual is embedded in daily life. The proverb *buna dabo naw* ("coffee is our bread") captures the degree to which coffee functions as daily sustenance and social fabric.

The ceremony typically unfolds as follows:

1. **Preparation of the space.** Fresh-cut grass (*ketema*) is spread on the floor, sometimes with flowers. Frankincense or myrrh incense is burned to purify the space.
2. **Roasting.** The host washes raw green coffee beans and roasts them on a flat clay pan (*menkeshkesh*) over an open flame, stirring constantly. As the beans crack and darken, they are held close to guests so the aroma can be appreciated.
3. **Grinding.** The roasted beans are ground by hand in a wooden mortar (*mukecha*) with a pestle (*zenezena*), producing a medium-to-fine grind.
4. **Brewing.** Ground coffee is added to a clay pot called a **jebena** — a distinctive vessel with a spherical base, narrow neck, pouring spout, and straw filter lid — filled with water and heated over charcoal or open flame.
5. **Serving.** Coffee is poured continuously into small handleless ceramic cups (*sini* or *finjal*) arranged on a round tray (*rekebot*), from a height of approximately 30 cm, filling each cup in a single motion.

**The three rounds:**

| Round | Amharic name | Meaning | Strength |
|---|---|---|---|
| First | *Abol* (አቦል) | Opening | Strongest |
| Second | *Tona* (ቶና) | Deepening | Medium |
| Third | *Baraka* (በረካ) | Blessing | Lightest |

Each successive round is brewed from the same grounds with fresh water. The third round, *baraka* (from the Arabic for "blessing"), marks a spiritual conclusion to the gathering. Leaving before *baraka* is considered impolite. The entire ceremony typically lasts 60–90 minutes and is as much about conversation and community as it is about the coffee itself.

The ceremony is traditionally led by a woman of the household and represents one of the most important social practices in Ethiopian domestic life.

## Processing Methods

Both **washed** and **natural (dry)** processing are widely practised in Ethiopia, and the distinction has a significant effect on cup character:

- **Washed processing** produces clarity, florality, and precise acidity — the profile most associated with Yirgacheffe and washed Sidama. Cherries are pulped and the beans are fermented in water tanks before drying on raised beds.
- **Natural processing** produces denser fruit character, jammy sweetness, and wine-like or berry intensity — characteristic of Harrar and natural Guji or Sidama lots. Whole cherries are dried on raised beds in the sun.
- **Anaerobic and other experimental methods** are an emerging category, particularly in Guji.
