# United States

The United States occupies a paradoxical position in the global coffee story: it is the world's largest single-country coffee consumer, a country whose modern café culture has been exported globally, and yet it grows almost none of its own coffee. More than any other nation, the US has shaped how coffee is experienced as a commodity, a lifestyle, and — through the specialty movement — as a craft. The three waves of American coffee history constitute a lens through which much of the modern global industry can be understood.

---

## Country Overview

The United States is a federal republic of 50 states and several territories, covering approximately 9.8 million km² across North America. It is the third-largest country in the world by both area and population, with approximately 335 million people as of 2025. The country spans six time zones on the contiguous mainland and extends into the Pacific (Hawaii) and subarctic (Alaska) regions, producing an enormous range of climates and geographies.

The US is the world's largest economy by nominal GDP and remains the dominant consumer market for most global agricultural commodities, including coffee. Its federal capital is **Washington D.C.**; its largest city is **New York City**.

## Coffee in the United States

### The Scale of Consumption

The United States is by far the largest single-nation importer and consumer of coffee in the world. Key figures from recent data:

- Americans consume approximately **26.1 million 60 kg bags** annually — around **15.3% of global consumption** ([Everyday People Coffee](https://www.everydaypeoplecoffeeandtea.com/blogs/news/coffee-statistics-2025-market-trends-consumption-data-consumer-insight)).
- **66% of American adults** drink coffee every day, with consumption at a 20-year high as of 2025, representing a 37% increase since 2004 ([NCA / Everyday People Coffee](https://www.everydaypeoplecoffeeandtea.com/blogs/news/coffee-statistics-2025-market-trends-consumption-data-consumer-insight)).
- The average American coffee drinker consumes approximately **3.1 cups per day**.
- The US specialty coffee market alone was estimated at **$47.8 billion in 2024**, with a projected CAGR of 9.5% through 2030.
- **45% of American adults** consumed specialty coffee on any given day, an 80% increase since 2011.

The US imports green coffee from across the globe; its largest green coffee suppliers by volume include **Brazil**, **Colombia**, **Guatemala**, **Honduras**, and **Peru** ([World Bank Trade Data](https://wits.worldbank.org/trade/comtrade/en/country/USA/year/2024/tradeflow/Imports/partner/ALL/product/090111)).

## A Brief History of Coffee in America

### Colonial Origins

Coffee arrived in the American colonies in the early 18th century, initially competing with tea as the dominant hot beverage. The pivotal shift came in December 1773 with the **Boston Tea Party**, when American colonists boarded three East India Company ships in Boston Harbour and dumped 342 chests of British tea into the water in protest of taxation without parliamentary representation. The act — and the broader political climate it reflected — made tea-drinking culturally synonymous with British loyalty. Coffee swiftly became the patriotic alternative, cementing a preference that would outlast the Revolution and define American drinking culture for centuries ([US Census Bureau](https://www.census.gov/)).

### Industrialisation and the First Wave

Commercial coffee roasting began in San Francisco in 1850 with the **Pioneer Steam Coffee and Spice Mills**, which by 1872 had become **Folger's Coffee**. In 1892, **Maxwell House** was founded, further industrialising the supply chain. Through the early and mid-20th century, coffee in America was driven by convenience, scale, and standardisation: vacuum-sealed tins, instant coffee (accelerated by demand during World War I and II), and the **electric drip coffee maker** (popularised by Mr. Coffee from the 1970s) defined the era.

This first wave prioritised accessibility over quality. Commodity-grade robusta and low-grade arabica blends, sold cheap and brewed in bulk, became the norm in American homes, diners, and workplaces. The diner culture of the 1950s and 1960s — bottomless, thin filter coffee in a ceramic mug — became the iconic image of American coffee. By the late 1980s, Maxwell House was the top-selling coffee in the country.

### The Second Wave: Espresso and the Café Experience

The second wave began in 1966 in Berkeley, California, when the Dutch-born roaster **Alfred Peet** opened **Peet's Coffee & Tea** — the first American café to focus on sourcing and roasting high-quality arabica beans with the craft attention Europeans applied to the subject. Peet trained a generation of American coffee professionals, including the three founders of Starbucks.

**Starbucks** was founded in 1971 at Seattle's Pike Place Market by Gordon Bowker, Jerry Baldwin, and Zev Siegl, initially as a specialty bean retailer rather than a café. The transformation into the café model the world knows came in 1983, when marketing executive **Howard Schultz** visited espresso bars in Milan and returned with a vision of coffee as a communal social experience. After Schultz acquired Starbucks in 1987, the company rapidly expanded, popularising espresso-based drinks — lattes, cappuccinos, macchiatos — that had been largely unknown to American consumers. Starbucks also introduced the concept of the **"third place"**: a space between home and work where people could linger, socialise, and belong.

By the 1990s, Starbucks had grown into a national and then global phenomenon, bringing dark-roasted espresso culture to mainstream American life. The second wave was characterised by an emphasis on the café experience, branded coffee culture, and the expansion of espresso vocabulary into everyday life — but quality was still subordinated to consistency and scale.

### The Third Wave: Coffee as Craft

The third wave emerged in the late 1990s and early 2000s, driven by a small number of independent roasters who rejected the commodity and brand-driven models of their predecessors. The defining companies of the early third wave were:

- **Intelligentsia Coffee** (Chicago, IL — founded 1995)
- **Stumptown Coffee Roasters** (Portland, OR — founded 1999)
- **Counter Culture Coffee** (Durham, NC — founded 1995)

These companies pioneered **direct trade** relationships with individual farms, treating coffee producers as partners rather than anonymous commodity suppliers. They emphasised **single-origin** coffees, **lighter roast profiles** that preserved rather than masked inherent flavour, and **precision brewing** — pour-over, Chemex, AeroPress, and later cold brew methods gained wide traction. Latte art, dialling in espresso by weight, and measuring extraction yield all became standard practice.

The third wave reframed coffee as an agricultural product whose terroir, variety, processing method, and roast profile all deserved the same kind of attention applied to wine or specialty food. It also introduced a new vocabulary — bloom, drawdown, TDS, extraction yield — and a new aesthetic: lighter, brighter, more transparent in sourcing and process.

The movement has since been absorbed, complicated, and partially commercialised. Peet's Coffee acquired both Stumptown and Intelligentsia in the 2010s; Nestlé and other multinationals have purchased other third-wave roasters. Some commentators describe a **fourth wave** emerging — characterised by scientific precision, fermentation experimentation, and the further blurring of coffee and fine dining — though this framing is contested.

## The Americano

A notable historical footnote: the drink now called the **Americano** owes its existence to American soldiers stationed in Italy during World War II who found Italian espresso too concentrated and asked for hot water to be added. This gave rise to a persistent stereotype of American coffee as "weak" — though the diluted-espresso Americano is in fact a quite different beast from the thin filter coffee of the first wave, and the caricature says more about cultural misunderstanding than brewing philosophy.

## Coffee Production in the United States

Despite its enormous consumption, the US grows virtually no coffee on the continental mainland. The continental US sits outside the tropics and lacks the elevation and climate conditions that arabica requires. The exceptions are:

### Hawaii

**Hawaii** is the only US state with a significant commercial coffee industry, and **Kona coffee** from the western slopes of the Big Island is among the most recognised and expensive coffees in the world.

- Coffee was introduced to Hawaii in 1828 by the Reverend Samuel Ruggles, working from cuttings believed to originate in Brazil.
- The **Kona Coffee Belt** on the western slopes of **Mauna Loa** sits at elevations of roughly 550–760 m (1,800–2,500 ft), with volcanic soil rich in minerals (potassium, calcium, phosphorus), reliable morning sun, afternoon cloud cover, and well-drained volcanic Vertisol.
- The dominant variety is **Typica**, hand-harvested on steep slopes that preclude mechanical picking.
- In the cup, Kona is typically clean, smooth, and moderately complex — floral aromatics, gentle citrus or hazelnut notes, low bitterness, and silky body. It is not a high-acidity or highly complex origin by specialty standards, but its terroir is genuine and consistent.
- Strict **"100% Kona" labelling laws** exist under Hawaii state law; blends containing as little as 10% Kona may be legally marketed as "Kona Blend," which has been a source of ongoing controversy and consumer confusion.

Hawaii also produces coffee in other regions — **Maui**, **Kauai**, **Molokai**, and the north shore of Oahu — each with distinct microclimates, though none carries the recognition of Kona.

### Puerto Rico

**Puerto Rico** (a US territory) has grown coffee since 1736 and was the world's sixth-largest coffee exporter as of 1890. Production declined sharply through the 20th century due to hurricanes, economic shifts, and competition from lower-cost origins. A specialty coffee revival has been underway since the 2010s, with farms producing small-batch arabica at elevation in the **Cordillera Central**. Hurricane Maria (2017) caused severe damage, but producers have largely rebuilt.

### California

A very small commercial coffee sector exists in southern **Santa Barbara County**, pioneered by Jay Ruskey of **Frinj Coffee** from 2002, and subsequently expanded to other growers. These coffees are expensive, micro-scale, and experimental in nature — more significant as a proof of concept than as a commercial origin.

## Coffee Culture

### The Drip Coffee Tradition

For most of the 20th century, American coffee culture was defined by the **electric drip brewer**: a medium-to-large batch of coffee made with pre-ground, often commodity-grade beans, kept warm on a hotplate, and consumed throughout the day. This utilitarian relationship with coffee — quantity and caffeine delivery over flavour — shaped the stereotype of American coffee abroad and established a national taste for lighter-bodied, lower-intensity brews relative to the espresso cultures of Southern Europe and the Middle East.

The diner, the office coffee pot, and the highway truck stop became culturally loaded archetypes of this era. "Just a cup of coffee" meant something specific: hot, black, thin, and free to refill.

### Espresso and the Specialised Café

Second- and third-wave culture dramatically changed the landscape. Cities including **Seattle**, **Portland**, **San Francisco**, **New York**, **Chicago**, **Los Angeles**, and **Austin** developed dense specialty café ecosystems that rival those of Melbourne or London in both quality and café-per-capita density. The Pacific Northwest — particularly Seattle and Portland — remains the spiritual heartland of American specialty coffee culture.

The café has also become a central institution of **remote work culture**. With the growth of freelance, gig, and distributed-team employment, coffee shops now function as de facto offices for a significant segment of the workforce, and the concept of the "third place" that Starbucks popularised has been thoroughly claimed by independent specialty cafés.

### Cold Brew and Ready-to-Drink

The United States pioneered **cold brew coffee** as a mainstream commercial product, and the category has grown dramatically since the 2010s. Ready-to-drink (RTD) cold brew is the fastest-growing segment of the US coffee market, with a projected CAGR of 6.1% through 2030. Canned and bottled coffee drinks, nitro cold brew on tap, and coffee-based energy drinks have expanded the consumption occasion well beyond the traditional hot mug at the breakfast table.

## Key Industry Organisations

- **Specialty Coffee Association (SCA)** — the primary international professional body for the specialty coffee industry, headquartered in the US; publishes grading standards, brewing protocols, and cupping methodology used worldwide.
- **National Coffee Association (NCA)** — the oldest and largest trade association for the US coffee industry; publishes the annual National Coffee Data Trends (NCDT) report.
