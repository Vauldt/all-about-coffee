## Overview

The EU Deforestation Regulation (EUDR), formally Regulation (EU) 2023/1115, is European Union legislation that prohibits the import and export of commodities and products associated with deforestation or forest degradation occurring after 31 December 2020\. Coffee is one of the seven key commodities covered by the regulation, alongside cattle, cocoa, palm oil, soya, wood, and rubber. The EUDR has significant implications for the global coffee \[\[Supply Chain\]\] and for Australian importers and roasters sourcing from affected regions.

## Background and Purpose

The EU introduced the EUDR in response to the accelerating destruction of forests worldwide, which drives biodiversity loss, contributes to climate change, and undermines carbon sinks. The regulation builds on the earlier EU Timber Regulation and extends its scope to agricultural commodities. Its goal is to ensure that products sold in the EU do not contribute to deforestation, effectively using the bloc's market power to drive global supply chain transformation.

## Scope and Coverage

The EUDR applies to:

- **Operators**: Companies that place regulated commodities on the EU market for the first time  
- **Traders**: Companies that supply regulated products further along the supply chain  
- **Products**: Finished goods derived from regulated commodities (e.g. roasted coffee, instant coffee, coffee extracts)

For coffee specifically, the regulation covers:  
 - Green coffee beans  
 - Roasted coffee  
 - Coffee husks and skins  
 - Coffee substitutes containing coffee  
 - Extracts and essences of coffee

## Due Diligence Requirements

Companies subject to the EUDR must conduct due diligence and submit declarations confirming that their products:

1. Are not produced on land deforested after 31 December 2020  
2. Have been produced in compliance with relevant laws of the country of production  
3. Are covered by a due diligence statement submitted to the EU Information System

Due diligence involves risk assessment and risk mitigation, including collecting geolocation data for the plots of land where commodities were produced. This requirement \- particularly the geolocation data requirement \- presents significant challenges for smallholder-dominated supply chains common in Ethiopian, Ugandan, and Indonesian coffee sectors.

## Implementation Timeline

The regulation was adopted in June 2023. After delays to allow industry preparation:

 -**Large companies**: Compliance required from 30 December 2024  
 - **SMEs (small and medium enterprises)**: Extended deadline to 30 June 2025

Countries are classified into three risk categories (low, standard, high) based on their deforestation risk, which affects the intensity of due diligence required.

## Impact on the Coffee Industry

The EUDR is reshaping how coffee is traded and sourced:

 - **Origin documentation**: Exporters must now provide farm-level geolocation data, creating significant traceability infrastructure requirements  
 - **Smallholder inclusion**: Smallholder farmers with fragmented landholdings face barriers to compliance, risking exclusion from EU supply chains  
 - **[Direct Trade](../specialty-coffee/direct-trade.md) relationships**: Roasters with direct relationships to farms are better positioned to gather required data  
 - **Certification schemes**: Existing schemes such as Rainforest Alliance and organic certification are being assessed for EUDR equivalence  
 - **Cost increases**: Compliance costs are expected to increase the cost of coffee entering the EU market

## Relevance for Australian Roasters

While Australian roasters are not directly subject to EU law, the EUDR is driving global supply chain transformation. Australian importers sourcing from EU-trading origins may find that origin suppliers increasingly require EUDR-compliant documentation from all buyers as a standard across the supply chain. Additionally, Australia is developing its own mandatory due diligence legislation, and the EUDR is regarded as a leading model.
