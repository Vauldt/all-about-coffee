# Direct Trade Definition

## Overview

Direct trade is a sourcing model in which a roaster or buyer purchases coffee directly from producers or producer cooperatives, bypassing traditional commodity brokers and importers. Unlike fair trade certification, direct trade carries no universal standards body or third-party verification — it is a relationship-based model governed by the terms the buyer and producer negotiate themselves. Roasters who practise genuine direct trade typically pay well above commodity and fair trade minimums, commit to multi-year purchasing agreements, and provide technical or agronomic support to producers. The model emerged prominently in the early-to-mid 2000s as part of the broader [Third Wave Coffee](../coffee-history/third-wave-coffee.md) movement.

## History and Development

The formalisation of direct trade relationships is closely associated with three American roasters: Counter Culture Coffee (Durham, North Carolina), Intelligentsia Coffee (Chicago), and Stumptown Coffee Roasters (Portland, Oregon). All three began developing farm-level relationships in the early 2000s, moving away from the broker-mediated import chain that had dominated specialty buying since the 1970s and 1980s.

Intelligentsia Coffee is frequently cited as the first roaster to trademark the term "Direct Trade" as a programme concept, establishing explicit written standards for what the relationship required — including minimum prices, farm visit obligations, and quality benchmarks. Their programme became a template that other roasters adapted or critiqued in the years that followed.

The model spread internationally through the World Barista Championship circuit and trade media, reaching roasters in Australia, the United Kingdom, Scandinavia, and Japan by the late 2000s.

## Key Features of a Direct Trade Relationship

A genuine direct trade relationship is generally understood to include the following elements, although no regulatory body enforces them:

- **Farm visits:** The roaster or buyer travels to the origin country and meets directly with the farmer or cooperative leadership, inspecting production conditions and quality processes on-site.
- **Direct negotiation:** Price is negotiated between roaster and producer without a broker acting as intermediary. This does not preclude the use of logistics importers for shipping and customs.
- **Premium pricing:** The negotiated price is substantially above the New York "C" commodity price and typically above fair trade minimums — commonly 25–100% or more above fair trade floor prices, depending on quality and market conditions.
- **Multi-year commitments:** The defining feature of most genuine direct trade programmes is the commitment to purchase from the same producer across multiple harvest cycles, providing the farmer with planning stability.
- **Technical assistance:** Roasters often share cupping feedback, processing recommendations, or agronomic knowledge with producing partners.
- **Quality transparency:** Both parties share data — the roaster provides cupping scores and market feedback; the producer provides processing records, harvest yields, and traceability documentation.

## Benefits for Producers

Producers who enter genuine direct trade relationships gain access to higher and more predictable prices than the commodity market provides. The multi-year commitment allows longer-term investment decisions — planting new varietals, upgrading processing equipment, or improving drying infrastructure — that would be financially risky without a guaranteed buyer. Agronomic and quality feedback from roasters can help producers improve cup scores over time, raising the value of future crops. Some relationships also provide access to pre-harvest financing, reducing dependence on local lenders.

## Benefits for Roasters

Roasters gain full traceability of their supply chain, enabling them to communicate the provenance of a coffee to their customers with confidence. They can set and enforce quality standards directly rather than relying on an importer's interpretation. Long-term relationships reduce the sourcing uncertainty that comes from re-buying on the spot market each year. The model also supports a marketing narrative of authenticity and accountability that resonates with specialty consumers.

## Criticisms and Limitations

The absence of universal standards is the most significant structural criticism of direct trade. Because any roaster can use the term without verification, "direct trade" has been applied to relationships that involve little more than occasional importer contact or purchasing through a broker who once visited a farm. The opacity of the model makes it difficult for consumers — or even well-intentioned roasters — to distinguish genuine relationships from marketing language.

Small roasters face a structural disadvantage: the purchase volumes required to make direct farm visits economically rational may exceed what a young roastery can commit to. A roaster buying five 60-kilogram bags per year from a farm cannot credibly offer the volume certainty that makes a multi-year commitment meaningful to the producer.

Power imbalances persist in many direct trade relationships even without broker involvement. The roaster typically controls the cupping assessment, sets quality thresholds, and determines whether a relationship continues. Producers in economically vulnerable positions may have limited leverage to negotiate terms, and the relational warmth of direct trade can obscure these structural inequalities.

## The Spectrum of Direct Trade Practice

In practice, direct trade relationships exist on a spectrum. Some roasters maintain genuinely close, multi-year, visit-based partnerships with individual producers. Others work through specialised green importers — such as Café Imports, Mercanta, or Sucafina Specialty — who themselves maintain farm-level relationships and provide detailed traceability; this is sometimes called "relationship coffee" to distinguish it from broker-mediated trade without implying the roaster visits farms directly. Some contracts labelled "direct trade" are broker-facilitated in logistics even if the price and terms were negotiated roaster-to-producer.

The honest framing is that direct trade is a set of practices and values rather than a certification — its integrity depends entirely on the honesty and resources of the parties involved.

## Contrast with Fair Trade

[Specialty vs Fair Trade](specialty-vs-fair-trade.md) covers this distinction in detail. In brief: fair trade is a third-party certification with an independent auditing body, regulated minimum price floors, and social premium requirements; direct trade is an unregulated relationship model. The two are not mutually exclusive — a producer cooperative can hold fair trade certification and also sell directly to a roaster under direct trade terms. Each model addresses different problems: fair trade establishes a floor and social accountability; direct trade pursues quality-linked premiums and personal accountability.

## Key Facts

- Direct trade as a formalised model emerged in the early-to-mid 2000s, led by Counter Culture Coffee, Intelligentsia Coffee, and Stumptown Coffee Roasters
- Intelligentsia Coffee was among the first to trademark and publish explicit standards for a "Direct Trade" programme
- No universal certification body or regulatory standard exists for the term "direct trade"
- Prices in genuine direct trade relationships are typically 25–100%+ above fair trade minimums, depending on quality and negotiation
- The multi-year purchase commitment is widely considered the defining feature that distinguishes genuine direct trade from spot buying with a farm visit
- The model differs from fair trade in that it carries no third-party social audit or mandated community premium
