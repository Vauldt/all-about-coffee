# Acreage

## Overview

Acreage refers to the total land area under coffee cultivation on a farm or plantation, typically expressed in hectares (ha) in metric contexts or acres in imperial usage. Farm size is a critical variable in coffee production: it influences yield capacity, processing infrastructure, labour requirements, access to certification schemes, and the farmer's negotiating position in the supply chain. The global coffee supply is produced across an enormous range of farm sizes — from smallholder plots of less than one hectare to large estates of hundreds or thousands of hectares.

## Units of Measurement

| Unit | Equivalent | Common Use |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Hectare (ha) | 10,000 m²; 2.47 acres | Standard metric unit; international trade and reporting |
| Acre | 0.405 ha | Used in older literature and some producing countries |
| Manzana | Varies by country (~0.7 ha in Central America) | Traditional Central American land measure |
| Cuerda | Varies by region | Traditional Guatemalan measure (~0.04–0.11 ha) |

Metric units (hectares) are the international standard for coffee production statistics and specialty trade documentation.

## Farm Size Categories

| Category | Typical Acreage | Context |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Smallholder | <2 ha | Majority of global coffee farmers; common in Ethiopia, Colombia, Central America |
| Small farm | 2–10 ha | Family farms; some hired labour; may have basic wet processing |
| Medium farm | 10–50 ha | Commercial operations; dedicated processing; may supply multiple buyers |
| Large estate / plantation | >50 ha | Corporate or cooperative operations; full processing infrastructure; export capacity |

The majority of the world's coffee — including most specialty-grade coffee — is produced by smallholder farmers with holdings of less than two hectares. In Ethiopia, average farm size is often under one hectare; in Brazil, large estates of hundreds of hectares are common.

## Acreage and Yield

Coffee yield per hectare varies significantly by variety, altitude, farming system, and inputs:

- **Arabica (conventional):** 500–800 kg green coffee per hectare
- **Arabica (improved varieties, well-managed):** 1,000–2,000 kg per hectare
- **Robusta:** 1,500–3,000 kg per hectare (higher-yielding; less altitude-sensitive)
- **Shade-grown / agroforestry:** Typically lower yield per hectare but higher cup quality potential and biodiversity benefit

A smallholder with one hectare of Arabica under conventional management may produce 500–800 kg of green coffee per year — an income level that places many producers below the living income threshold in their respective country contexts.

## Acreage and Certification

Farm size affects eligibility and viability for certification schemes:

- **Organic certification** — certification costs can be prohibitive for very small smallholders; cooperatives often certify collectively
- **Rainforest Alliance / UTZ** — certification available to smallholders; often group-certified through cooperatives
- **Fair Trade** — primarily cooperative-based; smallholders access Fair Trade pricing through cooperative membership
- **Cup of Excellence** — open to farms of any size; no acreage minimum; judged entirely on cup quality

## Acreage in Specialty Coffee Context

In specialty coffee sourcing, farm acreage is often listed on coffee packaging or green coffee lot descriptions as part of producer transparency. A micro-lot from a three-hectare smallholder farm is a common specialty coffee format — the small acreage and individual producer focus is considered a quality and traceability signal rather than a limitation.

> [!NOTE]
> Acreage alone does not predict quality. Some of the highest-scoring coffees in Cup of Excellence competitions come from smallholder farms of less than one hectare, while large estates may produce commodity-grade coffee at scale.

## Key Facts

- Coffee farm size ranges from smallholder plots under one hectare to large estates exceeding 1,000 hectares
- The majority of global coffee — including most specialty-grade coffee — is produced by smallholders with less than two hectares
- Metric units (hectares) are standard for international coffee production statistics; older literature uses acres
- Arabica yield ranges from 500–2,000 kg green coffee per hectare depending on variety, management, and altitude
- Small farm size can limit access to individual certification but cooperatives enable group certification for smallholders
- In specialty coffee, small acreage and individual producer identity are valued as traceability and quality signals
