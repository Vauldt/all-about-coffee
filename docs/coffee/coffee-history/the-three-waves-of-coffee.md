# The Three Waves of Coffee

*How coffee evolved from mass-produced commodity to craft beverage — and what each wave changed*

[Specialty Coffee Movement](../specialty-coffee/specialty-coffee-movement.md) | [Specialty_Coffee_Movement](../specialty-coffee/specialty-coffee-movement-2.md) | [Third Wave Coffee](third-wave-coffee.md) | [Specialty Coffee History](../specialty-coffee/specialty-coffee-history.md)

## Overview

The "waves" framework describes how coffee culture has evolved in three distinct phases, each representing a fundamental shift in how coffee was produced, sold, and understood. The model was popularised by Trish Rothgeb of Wrecking Ball Coffee in 2002 and has since become the standard lens for understanding the specialty coffee movement's development.

---

## First Wave (1800s–1960s): Coffee as Commodity

### Characteristics

- Coffee treated as an undifferentiated commodity
- Mass production and industrial distribution
- Convenience prioritised above all else
- Instant coffee as the defining innovation
- Supermarket brands dominate
- Dark roasting used to mask defects and ensure consistency
- No origin information or traceability
- "Coffee is coffee" — no quality differentiation

### Key Players

- **Folgers** and **Maxwell House** (USA) — supermarket-shelf dominance
- **Nescafé** — instant coffee globalisation
- Large industrial roasters supplying chain grocery
- Commodity trading systems (C-market) setting universal prices

### Cultural Impact

Coffee became a staple of Western households. Instant coffee made preparation trivial and universal. A coffee culture existed but quality was irrelevant — caffeine and habit were the product.

### The Problems Created

- Poor quality normalised across the market
- Farmers paid poverty wages with no quality incentive
- No mechanism to reward better growing or processing
- Environmental degradation from commodity farming practices
- Coffee viewed as simple fuel, not a craft product

---

## Second Wave (1960s–1990s): Coffee as Experience

### Characteristics

- Coffee repositioned as an experience, not just a beverage
- Coffeehouse culture revived and commercialised
- Espresso-based drinks popularised (lattes, cappuccinos)
- Origin awareness begins — "Colombian coffee" enters mainstream
- Darker roasting still dominant (French, Italian roast styles)
- Premium pricing established as viable
- Chain café expansion at scale
- Coffee as lifestyle and "third place"

### Key Players

**Alfred Peet and Peet's Coffee (Berkeley, 1966):**
Peet founded the first significant quality-focused roaster in the USA, importing high-quality beans and roasting with care. He trained the founders of Starbucks and influenced an entire generation of roasters. His philosophy: fresh-roasted coffee from quality beans, prepared with care.

**Starbucks (Seattle, 1971):**
Scaled coffeehouse culture globally. Made the espresso-based café drink mainstream. Created the "third place" concept — a comfortable, consistent social space that was neither home nor work. Quality remained secondary to experience and consistency.

**Independent coffeehouses:**
European café culture, particularly Italian espresso tradition, spread to North America and influenced the emerging coffeehouse model.

### Innovation

- Espresso-based drinks (lattes, cappuccinos) became mainstream consumption
- Coffee shops established as cultural meeting places
- Premium pricing accepted by consumers for the first time
- Whole bean sales increased alongside ground and instant
- Origin awareness began — origin names became marketing assets

### Cultural Impact

Coffee became worth paying £3+ for. Café culture expanded dramatically. The coffeehouse as social institution was established. But quality remained secondary to experience, and standardisation and convenience still dominated. Even premium chains treated coffee as a consistent commodity within a carefully managed brand.

### Limitations

- Dark roasting obscured origin characteristics rather than expressing them
- Standardisation over terroir — consistency required homogeneity
- Limited transparency in sourcing or pricing
- Coffee still understood as a consistent commodity, even if a premium one
- No direct relationship between quality and producer income

---

## Third Wave (2000s–Present): Coffee as Artisan Craft

### Characteristics

- Coffee treated as an artisan craft product, like wine or fine cuisine
- Light to medium roasting to express origin character
- Direct trade relationships with producers
- Full transparency — farm, processing, pricing
- Scientific, precision-focused brewing approach
- Barista positioned as craftsperson, not order-taker
- Single-origin focus with frequent rotation
- Competition culture as a driver of innovation
- Education emphasis throughout the supply chain
- Quality as a non-negotiable obsession

### Key Players

The third wave was defined by a generation of independent specialty roasters:

- **Intelligentsia** (Chicago, 1995) — direct trade pioneers
- **Counter Culture** (North Carolina, 1995) — sustainability and transparency leaders
- **Stumptown** (Portland, 1999) — quality and sourcing excellence
- **Blue Bottle** (San Francisco, 2002) — Japanese precision influence
- **Square Mile** (London, 2008) — competition and education focus
- **Tim Wendelboe** (Oslo, 2007) — Nordic ultra-light roasting philosophy
- Hundreds of independent specialty roasters globally from the mid-2000s onwards

### Philosophy

Coffee as a craft worthy of the same respect given to wine or fine cuisine. Origin matters. Processing matters. Roasting is both art and science. Brewing is precise craft. Relationships throughout the supply chain are essential, not incidental.

**The transformation in how coffee is described:**
- First wave: "Coffee"
- Second wave: "Colombian coffee"
- Third wave: "Natural process Yirgacheffe, Worka Chelbesa washing station, Gedeb district, lot 247, heirloom varietals, 2,100 metres elevation, harvest 2024"

### Impact

- Farmers receive higher prices — sometimes 5–10× the fair trade floor price
- Quality incentivised throughout the supply chain
- Transparency became an expectation, not a differentiator
- Education widespread — consumers, baristas, and producers all learning
- Home brewing renaissance enabled by affordable equipment and online education
- Competition culture flourished as a driver of innovation
- Coffee journalism and media emerged as a serious field

### The Ongoing Debate

Is lighter always better? The third wave positioned light roasting as the standard for quality expression. The honest answer is that light, medium, and dark roasting each have their place:

- **Light roast:** Best for origin expression, fruit and floral character, high-altitude varietals
- **Medium roast:** Balance of origin and development; accessible to more palates
- **Dark roast:** Roast-forward character; valid for specific applications; masks origin

The third wave's insistence on light roasting was a necessary overcorrection from the second wave's darkness, but the movement has matured toward a more nuanced view of what roast level serves the coffee best.

---

## The Fourth Wave — An Ongoing Debate

Several competing definitions of a "fourth wave" circulate. No clear consensus has emerged:

- **Science-focused:** Data-driven brewing, extraction science as the priority
- **Convenience:** Quality coffee made accessible — better instant, subscriptions, on-demand
- **Sustainability:** Climate and circular economy as baseline requirements, not aspirations
- **Technology:** Connected devices, AI-assisted roasting, digital traceability
- **Equity:** Economic and social justice throughout the supply chain as the central project

The current state of specialty coffee is still unfolding. What is clear is that quality is no longer the differentiator it once was — it is the baseline. The next phase builds on that foundation while confronting sustainability, equity, and climate resilience as defining challenges.
