# Origins of Coffee

## Overview

Coffee's documented history spans roughly six centuries of cultivation, trade, and cultural adoption, with legendary accounts extending further still. The story begins in the highlands of Ethiopia — the wild origin of *Coffea arabica* — passes through the Sufi monasteries and port cities of Yemen, spreads across the Ottoman and Persian worlds through the coffeehouse institution, reaches Europe in the 17th century, and is then transplanted to the tropical colonies of the great European powers, creating the plantation systems and commodity trade infrastructure that would eventually produce the first wave. By the mid-19th century, coffee had become one of the world's most traded commodities, grown across three continents and consumed across five.

## Legendary Ethiopian Origins

The most widely repeated account of coffee's discovery is the legend of Kaldi, an Ethiopian goatherd who, according to tradition, observed his goats behaving with unusual energy after browsing on the red cherries of a particular shrub. Kaldi reportedly brought the cherries to a local monastery, where monks made a drink from them and found it helped them remain alert during night prayers. The legend cannot be reliably dated and is not attested in any contemporary source; it was first recorded in writing in Arabic texts of the 15th and 16th centuries, possibly composed long after the events they describe. It is best treated as an origin myth rather than a historical account.

What is beyond serious dispute is that *Coffea arabica* is native to the highlands of southwestern Ethiopia — present-day Kaffa, Jimma, and Illubabor regions — where it grows wild in the forest understorey. Ethiopian communities have consumed coffee in various forms long before written records begin, including as a food (dried cherries eaten with animal fat), as a fermented drink made from the pulped fruit or dried leaves (known as *qishr* in Yemen and *buna qela* in Ethiopia), and eventually as a hot brewed beverage. The precise chronology of these practices is difficult to establish from surviving records, but the cultural depth of coffee in Ethiopian life is unambiguous.

## Yemen and the First Cultivated Coffee (15th Century)

The first clearly documented cultivation of coffee as a deliberate agricultural crop occurred in Yemen in the 15th century. Sufi mystics of the Shadhiliyya order are credited with the earliest recorded use of coffee as a brewed drink, consumed to sustain wakefulness during extended devotional prayers and *dhikr* ceremonies. The port city of Al-Makkha — known to Europeans as Mocha — became the primary export point for Yemeni coffee, and the name "mocha" became synonymous with coffee itself in European trade.

Yemeni farmers cultivated *Coffea arabica* plants brought from Ethiopia, terracing the mountainsides of the Haraz and Haraaz regions at altitudes of 1,500–2,500 metres. These high-altitude farms produced coffees with complex cup profiles — the genetic ancestors of the Yemeni varieties still grown today. Yemen maintained a near-complete monopoly on the global coffee trade for approximately two centuries, enforcing it by exporting only processed (dried or boiled) beans that could not be germinated, preventing the establishment of rival plantations.

## The Coffeehouse (Qahveh Khaneh) and Early Controversy

The coffeehouse — known as *qahveh khaneh* in Arabic and Persian — emerged in the cities of the Arabian Peninsula and the Ottoman Empire in the early 16th century. Mecca, Medina, Cairo, Damascus, and Constantinople each developed coffeehouse cultures in which men gathered to drink coffee, play chess, discuss politics and poetry, and conduct business. The coffeehouse became a significant social institution: a semi-public space that was neither the home nor the mosque, and that brought together men of different classes and occupations.

Coffee attracted controversy from the outset. In 1511, the Governor of Mecca, Kha'ir Beg, ordered coffeehouses closed and coffee prohibited, arguing that it produced a state of intoxication and encouraged seditious gatherings — a concern later shared by rulers across three continents. This ban was overturned within months by the Sultan in Cairo, but it established a pattern of official suspicion that would recur throughout coffee's early history. The Ottoman Sultan Murad IV banned coffee (and tobacco) under penalty of death in 1633. In Persia, Shah Abbas I alternately patronised and suppressed coffeehouses depending on the political climate. In each case, the bans proved unenforceable: coffee's social and economic function was too embedded to eliminate by decree.

## Arrival in Europe (17th Century)

Coffee reached Europe through the Venetian trade networks of the early 17th century. The first European coffeehouse opened in Venice around 1645; Caffè Florian, opened in 1720, is the oldest surviving coffeehouse in Europe. The Ottoman siege of Vienna in 1683 — and the legend of Franz George Kolschitzky, who reputedly claimed sacks of coffee abandoned by the retreating Ottoman army — provided one popular (if contested) explanation for the establishment of Viennese café culture, where coffee became inseparable from urban intellectual life.

England's coffeehouse culture developed with striking speed. The first English coffeehouse is generally dated to Oxford in 1652, established by Jacob, a Jewish man from Lebanon. By 1700, London had an estimated 2,000 coffeehouses, frequented by merchants, lawyers, clergy, scientists, and politicians. Because entry required only the penny admission fee, they were sometimes called "penny universities" — spaces where a working man could sit alongside a gentleman and hear the same conversation. Specific coffeehouses became associated with specific trades and professions:

- **Lloyd's Coffee House** (established ~1688) served marine insurers and ship owners; it eventually became Lloyd's of London
- **Jonathan's Coffee House** (established 1680) in Exchange Alley was where stockbrokers gathered; it became the London Stock Exchange
- **The Grecian Coffee House** was favoured by members of the Royal Society, including Isaac Newton and Edmond Halley
- **Will's Coffee House** in Covent Garden was the preferred haunt of writers, poets, and playwrights

King Charles II attempted to suppress English coffeehouses by royal proclamation in 1675, arguing they were "nurseries of sedition" where men wasted their time in idle gossip and political agitation. The proclamation was withdrawn within eleven days following widespread public opposition, demonstrating the depth to which the coffeehouse had embedded itself in English social life.

In France, the Café de Procope opened in Paris in 1686 and became one of the most celebrated intellectual gathering places in European history, patronised by Voltaire, Rousseau, Diderot, and later Napoleon Bonaparte. French café culture developed its own distinct character — more formal, more literary, more politically charged — compared to the English model.

## Breaking the Yemeni Monopoly

Yemen's two-century dominance of the global coffee trade ended when the Dutch succeeded in acquiring live coffee plants in the late 17th century. Dutch traders obtained seeds or seedlings from Yemen — accounts differ on the exact circumstances — and began cultivation in their colonial territories. The first successful Dutch coffee plantation was established in Batavia (present-day Jakarta) in Java, Indonesia, around 1699. Java coffee quickly became a significant source of supply for European markets, and the phrase "a cup of Java" entered common English usage as a synonym for coffee.

The Dutch also cultivated coffee in Ceylon (present-day Sri Lanka) and on the island of Surinam in South America. In 1706, the Dutch presented a coffee plant to the Hortus Botanicus botanical garden in Amsterdam; cuttings from this plant would go on to seed plantations across the world. A descendant of this Amsterdam plant was given to King Louis XIV of France and housed at the Jardin des Plantes in Paris — and it was from a cutting of this French plant that coffee cultivation in the Americas would ultimately derive.

## Coffee Reaches the Americas

The French naval officer Gabriel de Clieu transported a coffee seedling from Paris to the island of Martinique in 1720, reportedly protecting it from a hostile fellow passenger and ration shortages during the voyage by sharing his own water with the plant. The Martinique planting thrived, and cuttings spread across the French Caribbean. Saint-Domingue (present-day Haiti) became a major producer under French colonial rule, and by the late 18th century the Caribbean islands supplied a substantial proportion of the world's coffee.

Coffee reached mainland South America through a more colourful route. In 1727, the Portuguese colonial official Francisco de Mello Palheta was sent to French Guiana on a diplomatic mission to negotiate a border dispute. Using a romantic relationship with the wife of the French governor as cover, he acquired coffee seedlings — reportedly hidden in a bouquet of flowers presented to him on his departure. Palheta transported these plants to the Portuguese colony of Pará in northern Brazil. From this small beginning, Brazil would become the world's dominant coffee producer within a century, a position it has never relinquished.

## The Colonial Plantation System

The spread of coffee cultivation across the European colonies in the 18th and 19th centuries depended on enslaved and coerced labour. In Saint-Domingue, Java, Ceylon, and later Brazil, the expansion of coffee production was inseparable from the slave trade and systems of forced agricultural labour. The Haitian Revolution (1791–1804), the most successful slave revolt in history, was rooted in the plantation labour conditions of the Saint-Domingue coffee and sugar economy; the revolution devastated Haiti's coffee industry and created an opening that Brazil, still under the Portuguese crown, rapidly filled.

Brazil's coffee industry scaled through the 19th century using enslaved African labour until abolition in 1888, and thereafter through the immigration of European and Japanese contract labourers. The *fazenda* plantation system of São Paulo, Minas Gerais, and Rio de Janeiro produced coffee at a scale and cost that undercut all competitors. By the 1850s, Brazil accounted for more than half of the world's coffee supply; by the 1880s, that share approached 70 per cent. The sheer volume of Brazilian production drove down world prices, transforming coffee from a luxury accessible mainly to the urban middle class into a mass-market commodity.

## The C Market and Commodity Infrastructure

The collapse of Yemeni and then Caribbean supply dominance in the 18th and 19th centuries, combined with the explosive growth of Brazilian production, created the conditions for the modern commodity coffee trade. Coffee was traded speculatively on exchanges in Amsterdam, Hamburg, Havre, and New York; the New York Coffee Exchange was established in 1882 to manage the price volatility created by Brazilian supply swings. The exchange introduced the standardised futures contract that became the foundation of the global "C market" — the international benchmark price for commodity-grade Arabica coffee that remains in use today.

This commodity infrastructure shaped coffee's quality trajectory during the 19th century. As coffee was traded in large undifferentiated lots, evaluated primarily by grade and defect count rather than cup quality, incentives aligned toward volume and price competitiveness rather than flavour. Producers were rewarded for delivering commodity-grade coffee in large quantities; buyers had little mechanism to pay premiums for exceptional quality. The logic of the commodity market that the first wave inherited was established during this era.

## The Road to the First Wave

By the mid-19th century, the preconditions for the first wave were in place. Coffee was a global commodity produced at massive scale in Brazil, Central America, and Southeast Asia. Industrial roasting technology, developed during the latter half of the 19th century, enabled centralised production of pre-roasted coffee at scale. Folgers, founded in San Francisco in 1850, and Maxwell House, founded in Nashville in 1892, were among the earliest American brands to build national distribution around standardised, pre-roasted coffee. Hills Bros. introduced vacuum-sealed canning in 1900, solving the shelf-life problem that had previously required local roasting.

The first wave — the commodity and convenience era — did not represent a departure from the 19th-century trajectory so much as its industrial completion: the finishing of the transformation of coffee from a craft or semi-craft product into a standardised mass-market good. For a detailed account of that era, see [First Wave Coffee](first-wave-coffee.md).

## Key Facts

- *Coffea arabica* is native to the highland forests of southwestern Ethiopia (Kaffa, Jimma, Illubabor regions)
- The Kaldi legend is the most famous account of coffee's discovery but is unverifiable and treated as myth
- First documented cultivation: Yemen, 15th century, by Sufi monks of the Shadhiliyya order
- Al-Makkha (Mocha) was the world's first major coffee export port; the name became synonymous with coffee
- Yemen enforced a cultivation monopoly by exporting only processed beans for approximately two centuries
- First European coffeehouse: Venice, c. 1645; London's first, 1652; London had ~2,000 by 1700
- Lloyd's of London and the London Stock Exchange both originated as coffeehouses
- The Dutch broke the Yemeni monopoly with plantations in Java (~1699), Ceylon, and Surinam
- Francisco de Mello Palheta introduced coffee to Brazil in 1727; Brazil became the dominant producer by the 1850s
- New York Coffee Exchange established 1882; basis of the modern C market commodity benchmark
- Folgers (1850), Maxwell House (1892), Hills Bros. vacuum cans (1900) set the stage for the first wave
