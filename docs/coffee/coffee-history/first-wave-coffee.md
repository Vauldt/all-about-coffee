# First Wave Coffee

## Overview

The first wave of coffee refers to the mass commercialisation of coffee across the United States and much of the Western world, spanning roughly the 1860s through to the 1960s. This era transformed coffee from a loose commodity traded in bulk into a standardised household staple, sold in vacuum-sealed cans and instant powder. The defining priority of the first wave was convenience and affordability — quality, origin, and flavour complexity were secondary considerations at best. The commodity infrastructure and consumer expectations established during this period became the baseline that subsequent waves of coffee culture explicitly reacted against.

## The Commodity Era

Prior to the first wave, coffee in the United States was sold green and roasted at home or by local merchants. The industrial revolution brought centralised roasting at scale, enabling large companies to produce, package, and distribute roasted coffee nationally. Folgers, established in San Francisco in 1850, and Maxwell House, founded in Nashville in 1892, became the dominant mass-market brands of this era. Hills Bros., another San Francisco roaster, introduced vacuum-sealed canning in 1900, which extended the shelf life of pre-ground coffee dramatically and made nationwide distribution viable.

These brands competed almost entirely on price and convenience. To keep costs low, roasters blended beans from multiple origins and roasted them dark — a practice that masked the defects present in commodity-grade green coffee. The consumer received a uniform, consistent product with little variation from tin to tin, but with no traceability, no origin information, and no expectation of nuanced flavour.

## Instant Coffee and Wartime Standardisation

The invention of commercially viable instant coffee accelerated the first wave's reach. Sanka, a decaffeinated instant coffee, was introduced to the US market in the 1920s. Nescafé, developed by Nestlé, launched in 1938 and rapidly became a global product. During the Second World War, instant coffee was incorporated into US military rations, exposing an entire generation of soldiers to coffee as a standardised, functional beverage — hot, caffeinated, and reliably identical regardless of where it was prepared. Returning veterans brought this expectation of uniformity back into domestic life, entrenching instant and canned coffee as the norm for household consumption through the 1950s.

## Advertising and the Commodity Mindset

Mass-market advertising reinforced the first wave's values. Maxwell House's slogan "Good to the Last Drop" — reportedly coined in the early 20th century and attributed to President Theodore Roosevelt — positioned coffee as a straightforwardly satisfying household product, not a craft beverage requiring attention or expertise. Advertising copy of the era emphasised freshness (relative to pre-ground competitors), economy, and ease of preparation. Origin, varietals, and processing methods were entirely absent from consumer-facing communication.

The result was a coffee market in which brands competed on distribution reach, shelf positioning, and price point. Quality competition did not exist in a meaningful sense because quality itself was not a category consumers were taught to evaluate.

## Vacuum Canning Technology

The introduction of vacuum-packed canning technology was the logistical enabler of first wave dominance. By removing oxygen from sealed tins, roasters could significantly extend the life of pre-ground coffee — though the product continued to stale considerably compared to freshly roasted, freshly ground beans. The consumer had no reference point for comparison, and the convenience of pre-ground, shelf-stable coffee made the trade-off invisible. This technology allowed national brands to centralise roasting and reach grocery stores across the country without a local roasting presence.

## The Legacy and the Reaction

The first wave created the infrastructure — supply chains, commodity pricing mechanisms, consumer habits, and retail distribution networks — that the entire global coffee industry built upon. It also created the conditions for its own critique. By the 1960s, a small number of coffee professionals began to recognise that American commercial coffee had drifted far from the quality standards maintained in parts of Europe, particularly in the Netherlands, Italy, and Scandinavia. Alfred Peet, a Dutch immigrant who had trained in the European coffee trade, opened Peet's Coffee & Tea in Berkeley, California, in 1966 — an act widely regarded as the opening gesture of the [Second Wave Coffee](second-wave-coffee.md) movement. The second and third waves defined themselves partly by their rejection of the commodity values the first wave had normalised: anonymous origin, dark-roasted defects, and price as the primary measure of value.

## Key Facts

- Approximate period: 1860s–1960s
- Folgers established 1850; Maxwell House established 1892
- Hills Bros. introduced vacuum-sealed canned coffee in 1900
- Nescafé instant coffee launched in 1938
- Sanka decaffeinated instant coffee preceded Nescafé on the US market
- US military WWII rations included instant coffee, normalising the format for a generation
- Defining characteristics: commodity-grade beans, dark roasting to mask defects, pre-ground vacuum-packed format, no origin traceability
- Competition was entirely on price and convenience, not quality
