# Italian Roast

## Overview

Italian roast is the darkest of the commonly named commercial roast levels, characterised by bean temperatures at drop typically above 230 °C, Agtron Gourmet scores below approximately 25, heavy oil migration to the bean surface, and a cup profile dominated by intense bitterness, carbonised flavour compounds, and virtually no origin character. The name reflects the traditional Italian espresso tradition — particularly that of southern Italy, where espresso made from very darkly roasted blends (often including Robusta) has been the cultural norm for generations. In the specialty coffee era, Italian roast is rarely used by quality-focused roasters but remains commercially significant in Italian, southern European, and mass-market contexts globally.

## Defining Italian Roast

Italian roast represents the outer boundary of intentional coffee roasting — beyond which lies combustion rather than development:

| Level | Agtron Gourmet | Drop Conditions | Character |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| French | 25–35 | Second crack active; heavy oil | Smoky, bittersweet; origin absent |
| Italian | Below 25 | Very dark; maximum oil; second crack well past | Intensely bitter; carbonised; tar-like |

At Italian roast temperatures, cellular structures have largely collapsed, oils are fully migrated to the surface, and the carbon products of extensive pyrolysis dominate both colour and flavour.

## Chemistry

At Italian roast temperatures (typically 230–240 °C+ at drop), pyrolytic decomposition of melanoidins, polysaccharides, and protein structures produces:

- **Carbon compounds:** Graphitic carbon from extensive carbonisation begins to form at temperatures beyond French roast; Italian roast approaches this threshold
- **Charred organic compounds:** The characteristic "charcoal" and "tar" notes associated with Italian roast come from extensive pyrolytic breakdown products
- **Minimal acid:** All organic acids have degraded; the cup reads as flat, intensely bitter, and completely non-acidic
- **Reduced aromatic complexity:** Most of the volatile aromatic compounds that contribute to the distinctive aroma of lighter roasts have decomposed at Italian roast temperatures

## Traditional Italian Espresso Context

Traditional Italian espresso — particularly the Neapolitan and southern Italian style — uses very dark-roasted blends, often with significant Robusta content (15–30% or more). The Robusta's additional crema-forming capacity, combined with the very dark roast level, produces a distinctive espresso style that is intensely bitter, short-extracted, and traditionally served with sugar. This style remains the normative espresso reference in much of Italy, despite its departure from the lighter, origin-expressive approach of specialty coffee.

The specific Agtron target for "Italian espresso style" roasting varies by region — northern Italian espresso culture uses somewhat lighter roasts than the southern tradition — and the term "Italian roast" refers to an international perception of the style rather than a consistent Italian industry standard.

## Key Facts

- Agtron Gourmet below 25; drop temperature typically above 230 °C; second crack well past
- Darkest of the commonly named roast levels
- Bean surface: very heavy oil; dark near-black appearance
- Cup: intensely bitter, carbonised, tar-like; virtually no acidity; no origin character
- Traditional in southern Italian and Neapolitan espresso culture, often with Robusta blends
- Rarely used by specialty coffee roasters; remains commercially significant in European mass-market contexts
