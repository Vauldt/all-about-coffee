# Daily Caffeine Limits

## Overview

Regulatory and health authorities have established safe upper limits for daily caffeine consumption based on population-level evidence. The European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) and most equivalent agencies cite 400 mg of caffeine per day as the threshold below which adverse effects are unlikely in healthy adults — approximately four standard espresso shots or three to four filtered cups depending on brew strength and variety. Above this threshold, the risks of elevated heart rate, sleep disruption, anxiety, and gastrointestinal distress increase meaningfully for most people, though individual variation is substantial.

## Population-Level Guidance

The 400 mg figure represents a general upper limit for healthy adults, not an optimal consumption target. Context:

- **Espresso (single shot, 30 mL):** approximately 60–80 mg caffeine
- **Filtered coffee (240 mL cup):** approximately 80–120 mg caffeine depending on coffee type and brew method
- **Cold brew (240 mL):** approximately 100–200 mg depending on steep ratio and dilution
- **Robusta-dominant blends:** significantly higher caffeine than Arabica at equivalent volumes

The 400 mg daily limit therefore corresponds to roughly three to six standard coffee drinks depending on preparation type.

## Reduced Limits for Specific Populations

The ceiling is lower for several population groups:

- **Pregnant individuals:** 200 mg per day is the widely cited limit (EFSA, NHS, WHO); caffeine crosses the placental barrier and foetal metabolism is slower than adult metabolism
- **Adolescents:** Lower body weight and neurological sensitivity reduce the appropriate threshold; EFSA guidance suggests approximately 3 mg/kg body weight per day
- **People with anxiety disorders, hypertension, or cardiac arrhythmias:** Caffeine stimulates the sympathetic nervous system; individuals with these conditions should apply stricter personal limits
- **People taking certain medications:** Caffeine interacts with several drug classes including some antibiotics (ciprofloxacin), bronchodilators, and stimulant medications; consult prescribing information

## Genetic Variation and Caffeine Metabolism

Individual caffeine tolerance is partly genetic. The enzyme CYP1A2 is primarily responsible for hepatic caffeine metabolism; genetic polymorphisms in the CYP1A2 gene produce fast and slow metaboliser phenotypes:

- **Fast metabolisers:** Caffeine is cleared relatively quickly; lower cardiovascular risk at moderate-to-high intake; may require more caffeine for equivalent alertness effect
- **Slow metabolisers:** Caffeine remains in circulation longer; elevated cardiovascular risk at the same intake levels as fast metabolisers; more susceptible to sleep disruption, anxiety, and hypertension effects

Approximately 50% of the population are estimated to be slow metabolisers. Because CYP1A2 genotype testing is not routine, individual self-monitoring remains the practical tool.

## Individual Thresholds vs. Population Averages

The clinical 400 mg limit describes population averages, not individual optima. An amount that produces jitteriness, disrupted sleep, or elevated resting heart rate is effectively too much for that individual regardless of where it sits relative to the population threshold.

Self-monitoring indicators:
- **Sleep onset latency:** Difficulty falling asleep or lighter sleep in the hours after caffeine consumption suggests intake is exceeding personal tolerance at that timing
- **Resting heart rate:** Persistent elevation above individual baseline may indicate chronic over-consumption
- **Anxiety and restlessness:** Caffeine amplifies anxiety in susceptible individuals; if coffee consistently produces discomfort, intake or timing adjustment is warranted
- **Gastrointestinal effects:** Nausea, acid reflux, or loose stools following coffee consumption are common indicators of excess

## Timing and Accumulation

Caffeine's half-life in healthy adults is approximately 5–6 hours, meaning half of a 9 am dose remains active at 2–3 pm and a quarter is still active at 7–8 pm. Late-afternoon coffee consumption contributes to cumulative daily intake in ways that are often underestimated.

## Key Facts

- General upper limit for healthy adults: 400 mg caffeine per day (EFSA and most equivalent agencies); approximately three to six standard coffee drinks depending on preparation
- Reduced limits apply to pregnant individuals (200 mg), adolescents, and those with cardiac, anxiety, or hypertension conditions
- CYP1A2 genetic polymorphisms produce fast and slow caffeine metabolisers; slow metabolisers face higher cardiovascular risk at equivalent intake levels
- Caffeine half-life is approximately 5–6 hours; late afternoon consumption contributes meaningfully to total daily intake and sleep disruption
- Individual thresholds vary considerably; self-monitoring of sleep quality, anxiety, and resting heart rate is more practically informative than a fixed cup count
